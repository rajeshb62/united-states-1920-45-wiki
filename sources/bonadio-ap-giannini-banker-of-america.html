Overview

A. P. Giannini: Banker of America is Felice A. Bonadio’s narrative biography of Amadeo Peter Giannini (1870–1949), drawing heavily on Bank of America Archives (Bessie James collections), Bancroft and other California repositories, federal records, and interviews with Claire Giannini Hoffman and surviving executives. The book traces Giannini from Italian-immigrant San Jose and the Scatena waterfront produce firm through the 1904 Bank of Italy, branch-banking innovation, statewide consolidation politics, Wall Street entry under J. P. Morgan’s conditions, the Transamerica holding-company era, Depression survival and New Deal/Treasury fights, Hollywood and farm-country lending, World War II California mobilization, and Giannini’s embattled final years.

For this United States 1920–45 wiki the book covers Giannini’s life and institution beside The House of Morgan (Morgan–Giannini 1928) and Depression/New Deal banking sources: 1920s branch wars and the 1926 “Octopus,” Morgan rules and Ameritalia, crash and Transamerica, Walker/Monnet proxy crisis, Fed SF Calkins conflict, Morgenthau/comptroller branch-permit battles, Fleishhacker/Anglo-California and SEC pressure, and WWII/postwar branch freeze politics. Period-gate Prologue + Chs. 1–6 to brief continuity pointers; gate Epilogue beyond 1949 death/legacy except as a thin coda.

Book structure: List of Illustrations; Preface; Prologue; Chapters 1–18; Epilogue; Notes; Selected Bibliography; Index.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter / Contents / Illustrations

Lines 1–249. Jacket and title pages; University of California Press 1994 imprint and Library of Congress data; contents listing eighteen chapters plus Epilogue, Notes, Selected Bibliography, and Index; illustrations list (Swiss Hotel San Jose, North Beach, Bank of Italy license 1904, earthquake scenes, branch offices, Giannini family and executives, Transamerica proxy materials, wartime USS Portsmouth, funeral 1949).

Preface

Lines 250–362. Bonadio frames Giannini as a democratic branch banker whose 1909 San Jose office launched a forty-year expansion contested from Sacramento to Wall Street and Washington; credits Bank of America Archives, Bancroft, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, National Archives, Claire Giannini Hoffman interviews, and University of California Press editors.

Prologue

Lines 363–746. Opens on the October 1969 dedication of Bank of America Plaza and Giannini’s granite memorial; Claire Giannini Hoffman’s clash with the board over naming the headquarters after her father; family tragedy (hemophiliac sons Virgil and Lawrence Mario, Biff Hoffman); contrast between Giannini’s immigrant-era bank and pin-striped postwar management.

Chapter 1 — Waterfront Entrepreneur

Lines 747–1381. Luigi Giannini’s gold-rush immigration and marriage to Virginia DeMartini; A. P.’s childhood in San Jose; entry into Lorenzo “Boss” Scatena’s produce commission house; aggressive waterfront competition and reputation as “king of the San Francisco waterfront.”

Chapter 2 — A New Career

Lines 1382–1880. Giannini’s southern California produce routes and methods for breaking local wholesaler monopolies; 1899 political entry through Dr. Louis Bacigalupi and North Beach civic activism; decision to leave the thriving Scatena firm for banking.

Chapter 3 — A People’s Bank

Lines 1881–2271. Founding of Bank of Italy in 1904 in North Beach; multilingual tellers, door-to-door deposit solicitation, and “character loans”; April 1906 earthquake and fire—vault rescue and gold payments amid ruins; early real-estate and farm-community lending beyond San Francisco.

Chapter 4 — On the Move

Lines 2272–2701. Giannini’s foresight of panic and crash program to cut real-estate loans and swell deposits; board fights with Fagan; examiner friction and push to expand branches ahead of competitors after the post-earthquake recovery.

Chapter 5 — Going South

Lines 2702–3372. Los Angeles oil boom and Panama Canal immigration expectations; purchase of the Park Bank (1913) and entry into a Protestant-dominated “Big Seven” banking market; southern California branch building amid local civic resentment of San Francisco dominance.

Chapter 6 — A Storm of Opposition

Lines 3373–4036. Superintendent William Stern’s “house of cards” warnings and hostility to branch banking as “European”; sloppy examiner findings on rapid growth; 1915–19 legislative and independent-banker opposition; Giannini’s public defiance in Bankitaly Life.

Chapter 7 — Winning at Any Cost

Lines 4037–4726. Post-1920 Sacramento permit reopening and Central Valley branch seizures; big-city rivals Fleishhacker, Sartori, and Robinson convert to branch banking; California League of Independent Bankers oath and anti–Bank of Italy lobbying; Reverend Robert Schuler’s anti-Catholic radio campaign secretly financed by league rivals.

Chapter 8 — The Octopus

Lines 4727–5485. 1926 statewide consolidation politics: Young vs. Richardson Republican primary; John F. Neylan and Hearst connections; secret mobilization of 4,000 employees and 15,000 stockholders for Young; “de novo” rule and Johnson/Dodge banking-superintendent battles over Giannini’s “Octopus” expansion.

Chapter 9 — Morgan’s Rules

Lines 5486–6037. February 1928 cross-country trip to New York; purchase of Jonas’s Bank of America (New York) interest; J. P. Morgan’s conditions (Delafield retention, board veto); Belden intermediation; Banca d’America e d’Italia / Ameritalia ambitions and New York Federal Reserve scrutiny.

Chapter 10 — Season of Troubles

Lines 6038–6860. Giannini’s 1928 Rome visit, Fascist press praise, and lunch with Mussolini; polyneuritis illness at Lake Nemi and death rumors; May 1928 stock-market drop amid European currency speculation and growing speculative frenzy in Giannini affiliates.

Chapter 11 — The Downhill Slide

Lines 6861–7384. Spring 1928 Merchants National Trust (Los Angeles) purchase and the Hellman/Nolan/Bell group; Snyder’s warnings ignored; criminal activities among southern California executives; October 1929 collapse; formation of Transamerica Corporation (1931) as holding-company structure.

Chapter 12 — Showdown

Lines 7385–8165. August 1930 Walker and Monnet arrive in San Francisco; September 1930 merger creating Bank of America National Trust and Savings Association; Giannini’s failed backstage attempt to remove Walker and Monnet; Bacigalupi resists a “bloody battle” for corporate control.

Chapter 13 — Rough and Tumble in the Sunshine State

Lines 8166–8981. 1931 Associated Transamerica Stockholders proxy war against Walker/Monnet management; statewide Giannini campaigning vs. management “shock troops”; Mount’s centralized employee-proxy operation; Florida annual-meeting climax and Giannini’s declared victory.

Chapter 14 — A Narrow Escape

Lines 8982–10207. Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco governor Calkins’s harassment; Giannini’s 1932 Palace Hotel meeting with Roosevelt brokered by Joseph P. Kennedy; McAdoo/Scampini Democratic overtures; Depression survival tactics and Nolan/Hellman criminal fallout.

Chapter 15 — Unaccustomed Disappointments

Lines 10208–10945. Bank of Italy/Bank of America thirtieth-anniversary framing (1934); aggressive Hollywood lending to Selznick, Goldwyn, Disney (Snow White, Gone with the Wind); farmworker and populist attacks; Steinbeck’s Grapes of Wrath and Carey McWilliams’s Factories in the Field portrayals of “Bank of the West.”

Chapter 16 — Morgenthau Finds a Target

Lines 10946–11602. Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau and Comptroller of the Currency branch-permit denials (~1937–38); Giannini’s effort to distance Bank of America from agribusiness criticism; national press and campus protests; mounting federal scrutiny of Transamerica’s expansion.

Chapter 17 — “A Damnable Conspiracy”

Lines 11603–12295. Fleishhacker and Anglo-California Bank crisis under Morgenthau pressure; SEC complaint and public hearing (1940); Donald Richberg defense and Giannini PR blitz via Bernays; Giannini’s break with the New Deal and “Morgenthau-inspired conspiracy” rhetoric.

Chapter 18 — The Final Fight

Lines 12296–12956. Death of Giannini’s wife; Eccles/Patman holding-company hearings and branch freeze during WWII; California war mobilization lending; May 1945 resignation as chairman and Giannini Foundation gift; Morgenthau’s July 1945 resignation and first new branch permits in six years; frenzied postwar unit-bank acquisitions.

Epilogue

Lines 12957–13525. California’s 1900–1950 economic transformation; Giannini as architect of branch democratization; character as embattled “roughneck” outsider; June 9, 1949 funeral and legacy assessment; Fortune-era verdict on Giannini and twentieth-century growth.

Notes

Lines 13526–18634. Chapter-by-chapter endnotes citing Bank of America Archives, newspapers, state and federal records, interviews, and secondary works.

Selected Bibliography

Lines 18635–19120. Archival collections, newspapers, government documents, and published secondary sources used in research.

Index
