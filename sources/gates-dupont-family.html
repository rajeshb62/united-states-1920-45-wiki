Overview

John D. Gates’s The du Pont Family is an insider’s portrait of one of America’s richest and most private industrial dynasties. Gates, a du Pont in-law for many years and a Wilmington newspaper reporter, writes from a position of unusual access: several dozen du Ponts cooperated with the project, granting interviews and opening family perspectives that earlier books had not obtained. The book is explicitly anecdotal and personal rather than strictly scholarly — Gates compares his approach to Marquis James’s biography of Alfred I. du Pont — but it covers the full sweep of the family’s American history from their landing on January 1, 1800, through the 1970s.

The first half of the book is broadly chronological: Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours’s French origins, the family’s migration to America, the founding of the gunpowder mills on Brandywine Creek, expansion into dynamite and explosives, and the transformation of the company under Pierre S. du Pont into the world’s largest chemical corporation, with holdings in General Motors, U.S. Rubber, and other industrial giants. The second half is organized by family branch — Pierre’s relatives, T. C. du Pont, Alfred I. du Pont (the “rebel”), the heirs of “the Boss,” and various other branches — before concluding with chapters on the company itself and “a way of life.”

A central narrative thread is the decline of family control. The book opens with Irenee du Pont Jr.’s 1972 letter proposing the absorption of Christiana Securities (the family holding company, which held 28.3% of Du Pont common stock) into the Du Pont Company itself — effectively ending the family’s formal control of the enterprise. The antitrust case that forced divestiture of General Motors holdings (63 million shares) and the tax and marketability pressures on Christiana are treated as the terminal phase of a dynastic arc that began with E. I. du Pont’s powder mills in 1802.

For this United States 1920–45 wiki, mine the interwar company, GM, and family-control chapters. For the United States 1866–1919 wiki, the source is valuable for Gilded Age and Progressive Era industrial chemistry, the gunpowder and explosives industry, family-business governance through the holding-company form, the du Pont–GM connection, antitrust policy toward concentrated family wealth, and the social and political power of a single-family industrial empire based in a small state. Material after 1919 covers the later evolution of the company, family succession, and the 1960s–1970s dissolution of control; use those sections mainly for the long-term trajectory and afterlife of du Pont industrial power.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines 157–266. Gates explains his personal connection to the du Ponts (debutante party in 1957, marriage to a du Pont descendant, nine years as a Wilmington newspaper reporter). He contrasts his sympathetic perspective with the skeptical or hostile attitudes of fellow reporters. Describes the family’s studied avoidance of publicity and the cooperation of several dozen du Ponts in the project. Notes earlier books: Gerald Colby Zilg’s Behind the Nylon Curtain, Ralph Nader’s The Company State, Alfred Chandler and Stephen Salsbury’s Pierre S. du Pont and the Making of the Modern Corporation, and Marquis James’s Alfred I. du Pont, the Family Rebel.

Chapter 1 — Now and Then: An Overview (lines 369–1500+)

Opens with Irenee du Pont Jr.’s retirement in 1978 and the 1972 letter proposing Christiana Securities’ absorption into Du Pont. Christiana held 28.3% of Du Pont common stock and was the vehicle of family control; Du Pont once owned 38% of General Motors, and the 1949 antitrust suit (Attorney General Tom Clark: “the largest single concentration of industrial power in the United States”) forced GM divestiture. The chapter frames the book’s central theme: family control as a waning reality, Christiana as an albatross rather than a pendant of power, and the transformation of the du Ponts from a commanding dynasty to a dispersed, divided family.

Chapter 2 — The Move to America (lines ~1500–)

Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours (1739–1817), French physiocrat and politician, his friendship with Thomas Jefferson, the family’s escape from Revolutionary France, their arrival in Rhode Island on January 1, 1800, with one of the largest libraries ever brought to America by an immigrant family. Eleuthère Irénée du Pont’s (E. I. du Pont) decision to enter gunpowder manufacturing, the purchase of land on Brandywine Creek near Wilmington, Delaware, and the founding of the powder mills in 1802.

Chapter 3 — Building the Business (~lines –)

The early decades of the powder works, competition with established powder makers, the War of 1812 and government contracts, expansion through the nineteenth century, the introduction of dynamite and smokeless powder, and the company’s rise as the dominant American explosives manufacturer.

Chapter 4 — Two Crises (~lines –)

Two critical episodes: the powder-mill explosions that periodically devastated the Brandywine works, and the financial and operational crises that threatened the company’s survival in its early decades.

Chapter 5 — Pierre: Star of the Show (~lines –)

Pierre S. du Pont (1870–1954), the central figure of the family’s modern history. His education at MIT, his reorganization and modernization of the Du Pont Company after the 1902 crisis, the creation of the modern corporate structure, the expansion into chemicals, and the acquisition of a controlling interest in General Motors.

Chapter 6 — Two Brothers (~lines –)

Pierre’s brothers Irénée and Lammot du Pont, their roles in the company and the family, the division of responsibilities, and the dynamics of the fraternal partnership that ran Du Pont for decades.

Chapter 7 — Fathers and Sons (~lines –)

Generational relationships within the family, the pressures on du Pont sons to enter the family business, and the tension between dynastic expectation and individual ambition.

Chapter 8 — Pierre’s Other Relatives (~lines –)

The broader network of du Pont cousins and relations connected to Pierre S. du Pont’s branch, their roles in company and community, and the management of extended-family interests.

Chapter 9 — T.C. and Family (~lines –)

Thomas Coleman du Pont (T.C.), a cousin who was instrumental in the 1902 reorganization and later a U.S. Senator from Delaware. His branch’s history, political ambitions, and the 1920s conflict over family control.

Chapter 10 — The Rebel and His Relations (~lines –)

Alfred I. du Pont, the “family rebel,” who contested control after the death of the senior generation, fought legal battles over the family company, and built his own independent fortune in Florida banking and real estate.

Chapter 11 — Heirs of “the Boss” (~lines –)

The descendants of Eugene du Pont (the “Boss”), who ran the company in the late nineteenth century. Their inheritance, family position, and place in the expanded du Pont network.

Chapter 12 — A Diverse Branch (~lines –)

A lesser-known du Pont branch, their businesses and fortunes outside the main company, and the diversity of paths taken by family members.

Chapter 13 — The Underrated Victors (~lines –)

A branch of the family that built substantial independent success outside the central Du Pont Company narrative — their achievements, their relationship to the main family, and the family’s internal status hierarchies.

Chapter 14 — The In-Laws (~lines –)

The du Ponts who married into the family rather than being born into it — their roles, their acceptance (or lack thereof) in the dynastic structure, and the social dynamics of marriage into a closed wealthy family.

Chapter 15 — The Company (~lines 327–)

The Du Pont Company itself: its corporate structure, research and development, product diversification (explosives, paints, plastics, synthetic fibers — nylon, rayon, cellophane), the shift from a family-managed to a professionally-managed corporation, and the position of the family in the modern company.

Chapter 16 — A Way of Life (~lines 342–)

The social world of the du Ponts: chateaux-country estates, Wilmington society, the Brandywine landscape, charitable foundations, political influence in Delaware, and the texture of daily life in a family defined by inherited wealth and a shared name.

Appendix (~line 359 onwards)

Family charts and genealogical tables.

Coverage Guide

Gunpowder and explosives industry

Chapters 2–4 are the core material on the foundation of the du Pont powder mills, the early American gunpowder market, government contracts (War of 1812, Mexican-American War, Civil War), the transition from black powder to dynamite and smokeless powder, and the company’s quasi-monopoly position in American explosives by the late nineteenth century. This is the single most important source for the explosives and munitions side of Gilded Age industry.

Corporate reorganization and management

Chapter 5 (Pierre S. du Pont) and Chapter 15 (The Company) cover the 1902 crisis and reorganization, the creation of the modern divisional structure, scientific management, research, and diversification. Pierre S. du Pont’s MIT education and systematic approach transformed a family powder business into a diversified chemical corporation — a case study in Progressive Era corporate modernization.

Family holding company and control mechanisms

The Christiana Securities story runs through Chapters 1, 5, and 15. The family holding company as a vehicle for concentrated control, its tax advantages, the liquidity discount, the intercorporate dividends tax, and the 1972 decision to dissolve it are a unique window into dynastic wealth management in corporate form.

Antitrust, General Motors, and concentrated power

The 1949 antitrust suit (Chapter 1), the forced divestiture of 63 million GM shares, and the earlier acquisition of a 38% GM stake are central to understanding Progressive and mid-century antitrust policy toward family-controlled industrial concentrations. The du Pont–GM case is a major antitrust landmark.

Delaware and political power

The du Pont family’s relationship with Delaware — the “company state” — appears throughout the book: family members in the Senate (T.C. du Pont), governors (Pete du Pont), influence over state politics, the dominance of Wilmington by company employment and family wealth, and the broader question of concentrated private power in a small-state setting.

Family dynamics, intermarriage, and dynastic identity

Chapters 7, 14, and 16, supported by the individual branch chapters, treat the social and psychological dimensions of du Pont family life: cousin marriage (a repeated pattern), the pressure on sons to join the company, the management of reputation and publicity, and the internal status competition between branches.

Notes on Scope

This is a late-twentieth-century popular biography by an insider, not an academic monograph. It is anecdotal and personal in approach and largely sympathetic to the family. Its value lies in the access Gates obtained — interviews with several dozen du Ponts — and in its comprehensive sweep from 1800 to the 1970s. For this 1866–1919 wiki, the core chapters are 2–5 (business foundation and reorganization) and the early portions of 1, 15, and 16 (company structure and family control). Later chapters covering the 1920s–1970s are useful mainly for the long-term arc of dynastic decline and the antitrust aftermath.
