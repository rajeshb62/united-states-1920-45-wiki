Overview

Jennet Conant (granddaughter of Harvard president and wartime science administrator James B. Conant) approaches Alfred Lee Loomis through a family wound: the January 1940 suicide of her great-uncle William T. Richards, chemist and Loomis Laboratory consultant, and the posthumous novel Brain Waves and Death (as “Willard Rich”), a thinly veiled Tuxedo laboratory murder mystery. From Richards she opens onto Loomis—the secretive Wall Street power who built Tower House as a private “palace of science,” quit finance for experiment, and then, as war closed in, turned the same networks of money, Stimson friendship, and scientific hospitality into the organizational core of U.S. microwave radar (MIT Radiation Laboratory) and a Stimson-side “minister without portfolio” link into early atomic mobilization.

For this wiki the book is the private-capital / interwar lab → radar–atomic mobilization spine: Tuxedo Park society and Tower House pure science (ultrasonics, clocks, brain waves, guest star circuit); Bonbright utility finance and Crash survival; Lawrence cyclotrons and “big science” funding; Tizard Mission and the cavity magnetron; MIT Rad Lab under Loomis’s microwave committee; uranium-committee acceleration via Lawrence–Bush–Stimson channels; wartime radar applications (ASV, night fighting, Loran, GCA, invasion navigation); and postwar seclusion after divorce scandal. It pairs with Groves for MED administration and bomb delivery, with Elliott Roosevelt for high-diplomacy conferences, and with Allen’s Since Yesterday for 1930s public context. Treat as well-sourced secondary biography (2002) using Guerlac Rad Lab interviews, Stimson papers, scientific correspondence, and family material—not a contemporary 1920–45 primary; when building event pages, prefer cited primary speech over Conant’s narrative color alone.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines 186–435. ~1,390 words.

Opens on William Richards’s suicide (30 January 1940) on the eve of Brain Waves and Death—a pseudonymous novel set in a millionaire’s Tuxedo laboratory. Richards: accomplished chemist, Loomis Laboratory consultant, son of Nobel chemist Theodore William Richards, brother of Grace (Patty) Richards Conant (wife of James B. Conant, then Harvard president). Family culture equates anything short of extraordinary with failure; Richards’s gifts lay partly in music and art; he kills himself before forty. James Conant helps suppress publicity; the death becomes unspeakable. Jennet’s father keeps the novel and an unpublished short story on a uranium bomb. Authorial problem: manic-depression in the Richards–Conant line; grandmother’s destruction of incriminating papers; Loomis’s own erasure of the record. Research into Loomis to illuminate Richards yields the larger American story—gentleman science between the wars, then wartime transformation of that circle into radar and the bomb—and a parallel of family mental illness and Loomis’s bitter divorce/seclusion.

Chapter 1 — THE PATRON

Lines 436–1515. ~5,850 words.

Police find Richards dead in an East 83rd Street bathtub, wrists slashed; age thirty-nine; Loomis Laboratory consultant, former Princeton chemistry professor. Family hush-up; Harvard memorial notes on ten-year neurosis; Brain Waves and Death appears weeks later as “Willard Rich,” murder mystery in a modern lab. Draft short story “The Uranium Bomb” among papers. Conant uses Richards’s fiction and circle to introduce Tower House (1926–hastily shuttered 1940): Loomis hosts young scientists at a moment of transforming discoveries; within a few years that circle builds radar that alters the war and weapons that change the world. Tuxedo Park society backdrop; Loomis as patron who finances and shelters pure research before “real world” demand.

Chapter 2 — BRED IN THE BONE

Lines 1516–3078. ~8,700 words.

1946 Fortune portrait (no direct quotes/photos): Loomis indistinguishable at first from best-family, best-school vice presidents—until manners prove not the man. Intellect, versatility, fortune, scientific recognition, wartime accolades—all pursued unobtrusively. Family and education: prosperous middle class, Yale mathematics, law (Winthrop & Stimson), marriage into Farnsworth/Stimson orbit. World War I service: Sandy Hook then Aberdeen Proving Ground inventiveness on armaments and instrumentation (chronograph work). Friendship with R. W. Wood. Postwar restlessness: law feels like drudgery; determination to finance a life in experimental physics. Alvarez later: once Loomis tasted experimental physics, he set up his own laboratory at Tuxedo Park.

Chapter 3 — THE POWER BROKER

Lines 3079–4400. ~7,250 words.

Partnership with brother-in-law Landon K. Thorne; 1920 bloodless capture of Bonbright & Co. (“the deal by which the Bonbrights vanished from Bonbright”). Specialization in public-utility finance and holding-company architecture for electrification; Loomis rewrites “old school” methods. Fortune funds Tower House conversion into private lab. Weekend seminars; visiting Europeans (Bohr, Marconi, and others met at the boat and brought out). Einstein’s phrase: a “palace of science.” Domestic cost: absences from Club House Road home; three sons packed to boarding school on Yankee pattern.

Chapter 4 — PALACE OF SCIENCE

Lines 4401–6203. ~9,950 words.

By summer 1928 Loomis is press copy—the “fantastic dreamer incarnate,” ultrasonics as “whisper of death”—and recoils from Wood’s taste for limelight. Science boom and mass press; Tuxedo colony hostility and anti-Semitic edge toward “strange outlanders” at Gate Lodge. Research program with Wood and others: spectroscopy, high-speed photography/lightning (Boys camera), centrifuge-microscope with E. Newton Harvey, precision time. Double life: morning Wall Street throttle, evening/weekend lab. Crash aftermath: staff who played markets on dinner gossip lose savings; Loomis gives each $1,000 and lectures them never to invest on gossip. Critics fix on Loomis–Thorne continued profits amid despair.

Chapter 5 — CASH ON THE BARREL

Lines 6204–8229. ~11,160 words.

Post-Crash banking power: Bankers Trust, Central Hanover, First National; Loomis–Thorne among top financial interests. Stimson/Mills consultations; war-debt and European default crisis 1932; Loomis commutes to Washington in Hoover interregnum; Michigan bank holiday February 1933; Stimson diary on Loomis measures. Personal wealth enables pure patronage: anonymous support (e.g., Physical Review benefactor rumor), yachts, Hilton Head. Alvarez first hears of “mysterious millionaire physicist” and Tower House from Francis Jenkins; scientists as “barbarians who didn’t even dress for dinner” in park eyes.

Chapter 6 — RESTLESS ENERGY

Lines 8230–10065. ~10,090 words.

Mid-1930s signature ritual: “putting on the electrodes”—brain-wave/sleep research on houseguests, scientists, wives. Soundproof rooms, multi-amplifier pens on huge drum, oscillator tones, Hobart at controls; press “Svengali” coverage. Personal life: affair with Manette Hobart; signaling across Tuxedo Lake; household strain. Richards’s decline and drink; Kistiakowsky’s later guilt. Scientific turn toward microwaves and aircraft detection: Loomis drops other experiments; Tower House charter rewrites from pure science toward secret war-related radar technology. Bowles and early Army/MIT distance-finding contacts.

Chapter 7 — THE BIG MACHINE

Lines 10066–11591. ~8,480 words.

Ernest O. Lawrence at Tuxedo (~1936): cyclotron fame, boyish energy; bond with Loomis. High-voltage / atom-smashing lineage (Rutherford’s crack about American millionaires and a million volts). Loomis funds and accelerates large cyclotron plans; desk at Berkeley Rad Lab; investments and corporate door-opening (e.g., Stettinius). “Big science” patronage: industrial and philanthropic money, weeks that count as Europe deteriorates. Stimson’s early-1940 sense that war is coming soon shapes Loomis’s urgency on machines and materials bottlenecks.

Chapter 8 — ECHOES OF WAR

Lines 11592–13458. ~10,510 words.

Early 1940: Loomis drawn to Berkeley sixty-inch cyclotron and to Fermi (Columbia; slow neutrons; Nobel 1938). Bohr’s American announcements of Hahn–Strassmann fission; Meitner cable; Szilard’s chain-reaction vision and push for self-censorship after public discussion. Uranium implications against German lead. Domestic radar/microwave committee work stalls on power-tube limits—“write a report” as sign of not knowing next step. Climax setup: British Technical and Scientific Mission under Sir Henry Tizard arrives; Loomis to Washington; Tuxedo days ending.

Chapter 9 — PRECIOUS CARGO

Lines 13459–15733. ~12,770 words.

Summer 1940: Britain under air attack; Tizard lobbies full exchange of military-significant science; Watson-Watt initially dismissive of U.S. offer. Roosevelt, Stimson, Bush/NDRC path; Tizard meets Stimson (“offer us all of their secrets”). Mission cargo: cavity magnetron and related secrets (Cockcroft–Bowen worry whether the black box would sink if thrown overboard). Meetings with Compton, Loomis, Lawrence; decision to stand up a central microwave lab. Lawrence as chief recruiter. Site fight: Jewett/Bell vs MIT—MIT chosen after last-minute meeting. Anglo-American scientific cooperation as “quarrels of brothers”; Bush on deep friendships behind advanced weapons.

Chapter 10 — THE BLITZ

Lines 15734–17889. ~12,090 words.

11 November 1940: first radar-lab meeting, MIT Building 4—Loomis invitation “like a courteous order”; blacked windows; Rabi’s “same bar.” Karl Compton opens; microwave priority for night fighting: magnetron wavelengths below 10 cm for airborne sharp beams after Battle of Britain daylight success and night failure. Industry component sprint; AI sets, ASV, fire-control threads. Loomis style vs committee norms; Bowles friction. Bush grants latitude—Loomis unused to orders; “tough egg” who rarely heeded anyone overhead except wartime behests. Expansion toward operational systems as U.S. edges from aid into war.

Chapter 11 — MINISTER WITHOUT PORTFOLIO

Lines 17890–18818. ~5,000 words.

Spring 1941: physicists alarmed at Lyman Briggs uranium committee’s snail pace and material shortages; bomb whispers in Britain and America; German lead fear. They beg Loomis—radar lab jump-starter—to spur government. Plutonium/element 94 path: Lawrence, Compton, Segrè, cyclotrons to measure properties; Bush and National Academy review pressure. S-1 / OSRD meetings (e.g., Schenectady) on destructiveness, time, money; Oppenheimer estimates enter the room. Alvarez later: Loomis as Stimson’s minister without portfolio to Manhattan District scientific leadership—Lawrence, Compton, Fermi, Oppenheimer—because of mutual trust with the Secretary of War.

Chapter 12 — LAST OF THE GREAT AMATEURS

Lines 18819–21770. ~16,230 words.

Pearl Harbor: national radar unreadiness; son Henry (ensign on Pennsylvania) safe after days of silence. U-boat slaughter off East Coast and Gulf; microwave ASV turns the hunt (B-18 first sinkings; shipboard search radar). Alvarez GCA blind-landing—Loomis’s challenge keeps the project alive. Loran long-range navigation; invasion support (pathfinders, landing-craft control). Personal catastrophe: divorce from Ellen, marriage to Manette Hobart; social ruin narrative; Ellen’s collapse in family telling. Loomis refuses postwar limelight; “history forgot him” partly because he forgot himself—present and future over past. Patent note: Loran priority fights; patent issued to Loomis 28 April 1959 after appeals.

Epilogue

Lines 21771–22533. ~3,790 words.

Bush epigraph: only man who took Wall Street and also won National Academy election on physics accomplishments. 1950: declines Truman science-adviser feeler (William Golden); withdraws after divorce publicity. Continues funding colleagues’ projects (MIT, Berkeley, Cal Tech/DuBridge); astronomy/observatories. Oppenheimer security hearing shadow on Lawrence circle; Alvarez testimony; Chromatic Television with Lawrence/Gaither, rights later to Sony. Lawrence’s 1958 death; Loomis family aftermath (Ellen silence for years; Manette’s later remarriage). Closes with scientific publication list for Loomis (ultrasonics, EEG/sleep, time determination, etc.).

Author’s Note on Sources / Acknowledgments / Index

Patents (chronograph 1921; emulsions with Wood; microscope-centrifuge with Harvey; Loran 1959). Coverage Guide

Mine first as biography of Loomis and the Tuxedo→Rad Lab→atomic-brokerage arc, not as a general WWII survey. Chapters 3–6 for interwar finance + private lab culture; 7–8 for Lawrence, fission news, and pre-Tizard microwave push; 9–10 for Tizard Mission and MIT Rad Lab stand-up; 11 for uranium-committee acceleration and Stimson link; 12 for operational radar and personal aftermath. Preface + Ch. 1 for Richards/Brain Waves frame and Conant family position. Cross-check technical claims and exact meeting dates against Guerlac, official OSRD/NDRC histories, and FRUS/Stimson diary when locking event pages. Author is James B. Conant’s granddaughter—transparent stake, still secondary synthesis (2002).

Notes on Scope

Secondary historical biography (2002) built from archives, oral history, and family papers, written with novelistic scene-setting. Strong on Loomis’s dual career, Tuxedo lab culture, Tizard/Rad Lab origins, and Stimson-network politics of early radar and uranium mobilization. Weaker as sole authority on detailed combat operations, full MED administrative history (use Groves), or quantitative finance. Chapter epigraphs from Richards’s fiction are literary framing devices.
