Overview

Chernow’s The House of Morgan is a history of the Morgan banking dynasty and its institutional descendants: the London and New York Morgan partnerships, J. P. Morgan & Co., Morgan Grenfell, Morgan Guaranty, and Morgan Stanley. The book’s prologue presents the old House of Morgan as both private bank and quasi-public financial power: a firm that sold American securities in London, financed railroads and industrial combinations, rescued governments and markets, and generated a century of suspicion about banker influence.

For this United States 1920–45 wiki, mine Part Two from the postwar 1920s through New Deal reform (Crash, Depression, Glass-Steagall, Morgan Stanley). For the United States 1866–1919 wiki, Part One: The Baronial Age, 1838–1913 and the opening of Part Two through World War I finance are the core material. They cover George Peabody, Junius Morgan, J. P. Morgan Sr., railroad finance, the gold-standard rescue of 1895, the creation of U.S. Steel, the Panic of 1907, the Pujo investigation, Morgan’s death in 1913, the Federal Reserve transition, and World War I finance under J. P. Morgan Jr. Later sections extend far beyond the wiki’s chronological scope but are valuable for tracing the afterlife of Morgan institutions and the transformation from relationship banking to securities markets and casino finance.

The EPUB structure is: front matter; contents; prologue; Part One, “The Baronial Age, 1838–1913,” Chapters 1–8; Part Two, “The Diplomatic Age, 1913–1948,” Chapters 9–24; Part Three, “The Casino Age, 1948–1989,” Chapters 25–36; acknowledgments; notes; bibliography; index.

Coverage Guide

Morgan finance and Gilded Age corporate capitalism

Chapters 3–8 are the strongest material for the 1866–1919 wiki. They show how Morgan capital entered railroads, reorganizations, electrical companies, U.S. Steel, gold-standard rescue, and panic management. This source is especially useful beside Andrew Carnegie, because Morgan’s purchase of Carnegie Steel and formation of U.S. Steel is a hinge between entrepreneurial steel capitalism and banker-led corporate consolidation.

Private banking as quasi-public power

The prologue, Chapter 5, and Chapter 7 are key for a concept page on private finance before the Federal Reserve. Chernow stresses that Morgan’s firm sometimes acted like a central bank: defending the gold standard, organizing syndicates, and coordinating crisis response. This also makes the source important for understanding why Progressive Era critics spoke of a “money trust.”

Anglo-American finance and foreign policy

The prologue, Chapters 1–3, and Chapters 9–12 cover the London-New York axis: Peabody in London, Junius Morgan, Pierpont as Wall Street agent for British capital, and Jack Morgan’s World War I role financing Allied purchasing and loans. For the wiki’s 1914–1919 period, Chapter 10 is the central section.

Industrial combinations and Morgan clients

Chapters 4–6 link Morgan to Edison Electric, General Electric, railroads, U.S. Steel, AT&T, and other industrial giants. Use alongside The Wizard of Menlo Park, A Life of George Westinghouse, and Andrew Carnegie to map the financial layer behind electrical, railroad, and steel systems.

Progressive attack on banker power

Chapters 7–9 provide material for Pujo, panic reform, central banking, and public hostility toward concentrated financial authority. They also pair with Titan for comparison between oil trust/antitrust politics and finance-centered money-trust politics.
