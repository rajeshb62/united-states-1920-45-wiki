Overview

Barriers Down is Kent Cooper’s firsthand account of how The Associated Press—framed as a co-operative of American newspapers committed to truthful, unbiased news—fought for decades to end the exclusive territorial system of the great European proprietary agencies (Reuters of Britain, Havas of France, Wolff of Germany). Cooper begins with a September 1914 cable from La Nación of Buenos Aires seeking German war communiqués; that unanswered request, and Melville E. Stone’s explanation that AP’s contracts forbade South American service, launch what Cooper calls a crusade to liberalize international news exchange.

Writing in 1942, with a second world war under way, Cooper treats the cartel as a Victorian monopoly that partitioned the globe, flavored foreign news with national interest, and left AP contractually confined essentially to the United States and Canada while United Press (privately owned, for profit) could sell abroad without those fetters. The narrative runs through South American membership drives and Havas concessions; Versailles-era hopes for a free-press clause; postwar isolation of vanquished Wolff; Sir Roderick Jones’s Reuters rehabilitation; Japan and China (Kokusai then Nippon Shimbun Rengo); the 1927 Warsaw allied-agency meeting and League of Nations “Conference of Press Experts” at Geneva; the 1930 “Rubicon” plan for AP companies in Britain, France, and Germany; the Rengo–AP alliance and Reuters’s wrath; the 1934 AP–United Press anti-exclusivity pact and February emancipation from the four-party treaty; opening of Russia/Tass and non-exclusive contracts with Havas and DNB; Reuters’s wartime reorganization under British press ownership; and closing chapters on truth as propaganda’s answer and a different approach to a mutualized Reuters.

For this United States 1920–45 wiki the book is a primary media-and-diplomacy spine: interwar news monopoly politics, Latin America and Pacific agency contests, Geneva press idealism, emancipation of world news exchange, and the wartime claim that co-operative, unbiased news serves peace better than government or proprietary propaganda. Early cartel history (Victorian cables, Reuter/Havas/Wolff origins, AP’s “true and unbiased” moral concept) is continuity background for the 1920–45 fight.

Book structure: epigraph (“True and Unbiased News…”); dedication to AP employees and members; Contents (34 chapters); Chapters I–XXXIV; Index.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter / Contents / dedication

Lines 1–88. Copyright © 1942 by Kent Cooper; printed by J. J. Little and Ives, New York; all rights reserved; epigraph on true and unbiased news as America’s “highest original moral concept”; dedication to Associated Press employees and members; OCR-noisy Contents listing Chapters I–XXXIV (titles recoverable; page numerals often garbled).

Chapter I — A Crusade Is Born

Lines 89–305. Cooper presents The Associated Press as the story’s composite hero—a co-operative serving diversified member newspapers (including Hearst, Scripps-Howard, Gannett, and many singles)—contrasting it with profit agencies United Press and International News Service. He sketches courier-to-telegraph-to-cable news agency history, then opens the crusade on 8 September 1914 when La Nación cabled Melville E. Stone for German war communiqués; the unanswered request and the European monopoly’s contractual confinement of AP to North America set the book’s conflict, while United Press later served Buenos Aires without those barriers.

Chapter II — As Victoria Reigned

Lines 306–437. International electric communication annihilates distance; Britain’s cable network, preference rates for news, and Victorian empire practice give luster to the British cause. Paul Julius Reuter, German-born, naturalized in London, founds Reuters amid that cable system; French (Havas), German (Wolff), and later American cable laying follow, while the three European proprietary agencies pool resources into the great nineteenth-century international news monopoly.

Chapter III — A Great Moral Concept

Lines 438–678. Cooper, then chief of AP’s Traffic Department, finds the unanswered La Nación cable amid Marne-war flood and takes it to Stone, who says inquiries that cannot be answered favorably answer themselves—AP cannot serve La Nación. Shocked by the contractual explanation, Cooper frames a new crusade. He recounts how partisan nineteenth-century journalism plus telegraph cost-sharing forced AP members of differing opinions to agree on one thing: the co-operative must send only truthful, unbiased news—the “great moral concept” of the epigraph.

Chapter IV — Reuters Rex

Lines 679–1009. Origin legend of Reuters: carrier pigeons between capitals; Dover–Calais cable; London Stock Exchange and Paris Bourse quotations; approach to the Times to share continental news costs for private profit (unlike AP’s mutual, non-profit form). Cooper contrasts Reuters’s proprietary empire model with AP’s co-operative beginnings and traces how Reuters became overlord of international agency arrangements.

Chapter V — “We Are French”

Lines 1010–1359. La Nación’s appeal is explained: Havas, owning South American news rights, refused German communiqués because “We are French,” treating the continent as a news vassal of French wartime policy. Cooper argues AP’s contract made it party to suppression of legitimate news and that AP standards of availability and truth should prevail everywhere—not guarantee monopoly to any agency outside (or even inside) its home territory.

Chapter VI — Propaganda Aspirations

Lines 1360–1632. Breaking international barriers falls into three phases: South America, Europe, Far East. Nearly four years pass before AP moves firmly against Havas; United Press’s 1916 La Nación service (short-lived) pushes AP. After U.S. entry in April 1917, Washington presses for fairer presentation of American motives abroad; AP refuses participation in an American propaganda scheme for South America (Board debate 14 October 1917), insisting its crusade is to dredge propaganda out of channels feeding America, not reverse the flow for government publicity.

Chapter VII — Introducing Don Jorge Mitre

Lines 1633–1841. Don Jorge Mitre, director of La Nación and grandson of Bartolomé Mitre, comes to New York in 1918 seeking AP membership to escape Old World agencies’ “slow poison” of propaganda. A 1941 letter from Mitre recalls 1914 South American press as mere outlets of defective foreign services and describes Brazil and Argentina as conquered land for Havas propaganda purposes; war-coverage failure drives energetic action toward AP.

Chapter VIII — Toward a New Contract

Lines 1842–2103. Amid World War I disruption of agency relations (likened to 1942 conditions), Cooper wants AP to lead a postwar free international exchange instead of partitioned selfish interests. South America is the open test field against Havas; Japan/Western Pacific and a European agency union remain future aims. Stone values agency connection for efficiency; Cooper’s patience delays a suicidal break while he still shoots for the whole world.

Chapter IX — The First Concession

Lines 2104–2454. Cooper sails for South America in summer 1918 (submarine campaign; Spanish practice; friendship with Roy Howard of United Press). President Frank B. Noyes prefers that Cooper, not Stone, take the trip. Negotiations and fieldwork yield the first practical concession toward AP membership and service on the continent despite unfinished Havas contractual settlement; Cooper exposes a false “Prensa Asociada” giving pro-German slant under AP’s name and urges direct membership with La Nación, La Prensa, La Razón, and west-coast papers.

Chapter X — Reward of Merit

Lines 2455–2639. At the 22 April 1919 AP annual luncheon (Waldorf-Astoria), President Noyes celebrates Pan-American harvest: from 1 January 1919 AP received important South American and Panama newspapers into membership and began service (lists Buenos Aires, Rio, Chilean, Ecuadorian, Panama, Lima papers). Noyes praises strengthened friendship and commerce over propaganda; Cooper’s negotiation success is announced despite delayed arrival from the continent—reward of the South American policy.

Chapter XI — Peace Depends Upon a Free Press

Lines 2640–2917. Encouraged by board praise, Cooper argues AP’s foreign dissemination must not stop at South America and prepares for a possible agency break. May 1919 trip to Paris impresses him with peace-treaty methods and the absence of any free-press / free international news-exchange article amid Utopian welfare talk. He meets Sir Roderick Jones of Reuters in London (after Germany), fails to convert Jones to freedom-of-news idealism, and frames the thesis that world peace depends on a free press and unbiased news.

Chapter XII — Isolating the Vanquished

Lines 2918–3314. Spring 1920: Stone reports to the board on postwar European agency relations after the peace treaty. Board sends Stone abroad for a new treaty with Reuters, Havas, and Wolff seeking freer dealings with individual papers and co-operative agencies; the “big three” (Wolff resumed only toward AP) send him home empty-handed under Sir Roderick Jones’s command. Cooper recounts the isolation of vanquished German Wolff and Reuters–Havas consolidation of overlordship versus AP aspirations.

Chapter XIII — A Knight Convinced

Lines 3315–3636. While the board debates mutual co-operation abroad, Sir Roderick rehabilitates Reuters and tightens overseas dominions; fall 1920 world tour begins with New York, where he seeks higher payments and meets Noyes and the board (Cooper now assistant general manager). Noyes envelops Jones with the non-profit co-operative gospel; Cooper watches the “knight” of Reuters pressed toward press ownership ideals even as Jones remains determined to make Reuters immensely profitable.

Chapter XIV — In the Western Pacific

Lines 3637–3842. At board solicitation, Noyes goes to Japan and China in 1922; Director McClatchy, long favoring Japan service, warns that AP has an undesirable Western Pacific reputation and is misrepresented because it takes much Japanese/Chinese news through Reuters (itself alleged to buy biased local “stuff”). Revolt against Reuters overlordship in the Pacific becomes an indirect cause accelerating AP’s drive to remove foreign-field restrictions.

Chapter XV — Without Government Influence

Lines 3843–4040. Fall 1925: AP policy hardens toward European overlordship; Cooper becomes general manager in April. Ochs voices free-press-for-peace ideas—encourage agencies not dominated by privately owned corporations, free circulation of news free from government influence. Houssaye of Havas remains cordial but immovable (“Nothing can change Havas”); Cooper notes Havas disappears under German occupation in 1940. Title of general manager improves negotiation entrée with Jones and the cartel.

Chapter XVI — A Great President

Lines 4041–4389. Noyes, acknowledging Cooper’s more radical revisionism, insists the general manager present his full recommendations to the board rather than withhold them out of deference—model of co-operative presidential trust. Cooper urges dealing more directly with “subordinate” national agencies and newspapers rather than only through Reuters’s control; Ochs summarizes the issue as escaping Reuters mediation to reach local presses with AP’s “leaven.”

Chapter XVII — Nippon Shimbun Rengo

Lines 4390–4708. Noyes’s 1922 Japan/China findings: officials care more about exporting Japan’s merits than receiving American news; Kokusai stock is held by Japanese businessmen aiming at a national agency like Havas/Reuters, with eventual newspaper takeover on AP lines likely. Noyes teaches AP principles in high places; contrast with Jones’s earlier tour to clinch Reuters’s hold. Later evolution toward Nippon Shimbun Rengo as Japanese press co-operative prepares the Rengo–AP alignment.

Chapter XVIII — “Conference of Press Experts”

Lines 4709–5159. Summer 1927: Cooper tours sixteen European countries and attends the allied agencies’ Warsaw conference (postponed for his arrival); Jones entertains him in London with cabinet and publisher guests. Warsaw shows Reuters–Havas dominance of the twenty-nine-agency alliance. At Geneva’s League of Nations Conference of Press Experts, Cooper pushes protection for truthful international news exchange; Lord Riddell’s skepticism underlines how hard idealism is against national interests—Cooper fears another upheaval before AP principles flower.

Chapter XIX — A Step Towards Freedom

Lines 5160–5382. Concurrent with Geneva, a new Reuters–Havas–Wolff–AP contract takes form—first basic change in thirty-four years—but must remain acceptable to the Europeans. Jones senses AP will not break; the instrument is what he will give under argument. Cooper is irked by the prior Reuters–Havas world division that leaves him to retire while the three confer jointly against AP (as with Stone in 1920). Partial broadening is a step toward freedom, not emancipation.

Chapter XX — Reuters Hears From Japan

Lines 5383–5594. From October 1927 to spring 1929, demand for AP service rises from Reuters territory—India, Australia, China, Japan, even England. Rengo (succeeding Kokusai from 1926) edges toward AP; Iwanaga pledges Rengo ally of AP first, yet remains financially burdened by Reuters payments (installments on Kokusai rights plus large yearly service charges). Geneva letters give AP only limited “free entry” into Japan under Reuters-framed wording—insufficient for a free hand.

Chapter XXI — Volcanic Material

Lines 5595–5788. Board debate intensifies. Colonel Robert R. McCormick argues nationalist agencies (Reuters, Havas, German) are inevitably propaganda agencies; American press stature and revenue now justify AP world gathering of news as a patriotic object so foreign readers get more than murders, lynchings, and wrecks. “Volcanic” board language pushes toward decisive foreign expansion within or despite contract obligations.

Chapter XXII — Crossing the Rubicon

Lines 5789–6172. After a 1930 European study trip, Cooper on 30 September 1930 recommends creating Associated Press of Great Britain (with Paris branch) and AP in Germany as springboards for foreign activity (interim: collect news photos for AP’s new photo service). He holds that agency relations now disadvantage AP and that AP can stand alone if severed. Sixteen years after the crusade began, he risks board rejection rather than leave the world’s largest association harnessed to Reuters’s monopoly.

Chapter XXIII — Reuters Gets A Shock

Lines 6173–6422. Directors applaud Cooper’s statement—nearly unprecedented—and approve the far-reaching recommendation after extended inquiry. Ochs enthusiastically backs free hand and organizations in England, France, and Germany, offering financial help; McCormick favors it and contrasts European trade-propaganda news taste with American news standards. Reuters faces the shock of AP’s resolved entry program.

Chapter XXIV — Around the World to London

Lines 6423–6785. On 8 May 1932 Iwanaga gives Reuters one-year notice to terminate (effective 30 June 1933), seeking treaty change not abrupt break, and intends a world-outside-Japan/China coverage agreement with AP from 1 July 1933. Both men hope Reuters may someday be newspaper-managed like AP and Rengo so international news is not trafficked for profit. Ferment follows; three-cornered Rengo–Reuters–AP equality eludes Jones; Cooper’s path runs around the world toward London confrontation.

Chapter XXV — Reuters in Wrath

Lines 6786–7090. Sir Roderick bitterly indignant over the AP–Rengo contract; while Cooper is at sea from Japan, Reuters serves cold formal notice to terminate the four-party treaty “to clear the way for modifications.” In London Jones is outwardly Chesterfieldian—club, country weekend—but does not introduce Cooper to Press Association directors (owners of Reuters), though Jones when in New York sat with AP’s full board. Imminent break between the two countries’ press institutions sharpens the wrath beneath courtesy.

Chapter XXVI — An Answer Is Given

Lines 7091–7421. Winter 1933: Reuters brings the monopoly-vs.–New World antithesis to a head, proposing to penalize AP with a large differential payment for access to Reuters service (differentials had ended with the 1927 basic change). Cooper stresses AP’s greater resources, Canadian Press adherence, Havas’s opened South America, dominion papers seeking AP, and Rengo’s five-year AP bond—yet Reuters still demands penalty. AP’s answer rejects the Victorian exclusive principle.

Chapter XXVII — Melodrama by the Thames

Lines 7422–7706. European stir turns melodramatic: foggy Fleet Street café scene in which Reuters secretary Murray tips United Press’s Lorin Johnson that Karl Bickel should rush to London to displace AP with Reuters. Cooper trusts Bickel will not—shared Geneva 1927 code principles; UP grew without embracing Reuters monopoly. Recounts UP leadership lineage and competitive but non-monopoly American agency ethos amid Thames intrigue.

Chapter XXVIII — A Stroke Against Monopoly

Lines 7707–8046. While Jones rushes to America, AP and United Press sign a unique treaty at Noyes’s Ritz-Carlton rooms (Noyes and Cooper; Bickel and Hawkins; lawyers John W. Davis and Thomas Sidlo): not exclusivity but a pledge that neither will seek or accept it, door open to International News Service if it signs. Preamble condemns European exclusive arrangements, rejects selfish advantage, and unites for freer flow of European and British Isles news—a stroke against the Victorian monopoly hardened at Versailles-era contracts.

Chapter XXIX — Emancipation!

Lines 8047–8303. Emancipation falls on Lincoln’s birthday, 12 February 1934 (after 10 February Noyes–Jones conference). Cooper refuses continuance of the four-party treaty; AP wants direct two-party contracts without third-agency consent—end of Reuters overlordship—and prefers a direct Press Association link for a modern coalition of principles. Formal break with exclusive cartel structure emancipates AP and later other agencies under Reuters contracts.

Chapter XXX — Russia Opens Its Doors

Lines 8304–8565. Post-signing, Cooper sails as far as Moscow with Lloyd Stratton to restore Havas–DNB relations and explain AP’s purpose; Stratton negotiates a non-exclusive AP–Havas treaty parallel to Reuters (Houssaye heartburnings after having preferred AP over Reuters in the break). Moscow landmark: non-exclusivity policy as United Press also seeks Tass; Russia’s doors open on open-door terms. Reuters, Havas, and DNB strategically retreat from world exclusivity outside European/British spheres; Cooper’s principle that no agency own foreign nations’ news rights advances.

Chapter XXXI — Reuters Takes New Form

Lines 8566–8975. Open door lets Reuters sell to New York Times, U.S. radio, even AP competitors, and enter South America—needed revenue as war cuts continental income and empire troubles mount. AP does not suffer from sharing Reuters. February 1941: Sir Roderick lays down administration; Press Association acquires his shares; Samuel Storey briefly chairs; war and AP’s stand strip exclusive contracts. Jones’s later Times letter claims decades of striving for complete British press ownership of Reuters (offer to newspapers 1925), echoing Noyes’s 1920 mutualization gospel.

Chapter XXXII — Truth—The Best Propaganda

Lines 8976–9258. After two years of war, British press co-operatively takes charge of its principal news service (1941); America had shown newspaper ownership of the national agency for nearly a century. Commons talk of government control of Reuters is declined—only press ownership is sound. With AP serving British papers after the ban lifts, government places no wartime foreign-propaganda demand on Reuters comparable to older wars. Cooper’s letter to a U.S. defense/propaganda official defends AP’s Latin American work (begun to displace German propaganda) as more valuable than committee publicity—truth as the best propaganda.

Chapter XXXIII — A Different Approach

Lines 9259–9714. May 1942: W. J. Haley of Reuters’s new board visits New York to explain press-trust changes and seek a contract matching the new Reuters personality. Cooper’s prepared paragraphs date Press Association stock control to 1925 under Jones’s management, Jones’s 1941 retirement, then equal London/provincial trust control acceptable to government—no single autocrat. Correspondence with Haley sketches unfettered exchange of truthful, unbiased news as the basis for any new AP–Reuters phase, with or without contract, against favoritism and government support in news.

Chapter XXXIV — Retrospect

Lines 9715–9908. Closing meditation: impetus to act comes from what one observes, hears, or reads. After Versailles disappointment over no free-press article, Cooper’s Paris Bastille Day 1919 parade under the Arc de Triomphe—wounded first, then Foch, Americans under Pershing, Allies alphabetically—stamps the cost of war and renews resolve for free international news as peace’s neglected foundation. Retrospect gathers the crusade’s meaning for 1942 readers.
