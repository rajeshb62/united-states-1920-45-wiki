Overview

Lansing, Wilson’s Secretary of State and a Commissioner Plenipotentiary to Negotiate Peace, writes after Wilson’s letter of 11 February 1920 asked him to resign for “reluctance and divergence of judgment.” He tenders the office the next day. The book is not a full history of the Paris Conference. It is a brief of the subjects on which the two men differed from the October 1918 armistice talks through delivery of the German treaty on 7 May 1919, with a coda on William C. Bullitt’s September 1919 Senate testimony.

For this wiki the source is the American interior of the peace that the 1920s inherited: Article 10 as the Senate’s target, the unratified French assistance treaty, Shantung as a Far Eastern grievance, secret Councils of Ten and Four as the method later blamed for the treaty’s reception, and Lansing’s February 1920 fall as the last official act of the Wilson peace. Pair it with Recent History of the United States Chapters XXV–XXVII for demobilization and normalcy, and with Only Yesterday for the public League fight, Red Scare onset, and the Post-war Decade’s opening. The Economic Pinch (1923) is a different postwar brief—domestic deflation and privilege—against which Lansing’s diplomatic narrative can be set.

Narrative Source Flow

Front Matter

Lines 1–273. Cover and title pages identify The Peace Negotiations: A Personal Narrative by Robert Lansing, Houghton Mifflin, 1921. Copyright is reserved under the 1914 convention for named Latin American states. Contents lists nineteen chapters, a Conclusion, and six appendices. Illustrations include the American delegation at Paris (House, Lansing, Wilson, White, Bliss), Lansing’s commission, Wilson’s arrival on the Rue Royale, 14 December 1918, the delegation and staff, a Quai d’Orsay meeting after Wilson’s departure, Lansing’s “full powers” for the French assistance treaty, and the daily conference of the American Peace Commission.

Chapter I — Reasons for Writing a Personal Narrative

Lines 275–623. Opens with Wilson’s 11 February 1920 letter asking Lansing to give up the office because he accepted presidential guidance only with increasing reluctance, and with Lansing’s 12 February resignation letter dating the breach from January 1919. Lansing states that propriety kept him silent until a new administration was in power; the American people are entitled to know the subjects of disagreement over the Treaty of Versailles. He limits the narrative to those differences, not to a complete conference diary.

Chapter II — Mr. Wilson’s Presence at the Peace Conference

Lines 624–1069. October 1918: Austria-Hungary’s peace overtures, the Bulgarian armistice, Turkish collapse, and the German armistice of 11 November. Wilson sends Colonel Edward M. House as Special Commissioner to the Supreme War Council (Clemenceau, Lloyd George, Orlando). House tells Lansing that both will be plenipotentiaries and that Wilson is considering attending in person. Lansing argues against a sitting President at the conference: it mixes the office with delegate bargaining, weakens the Secretary of State, and exposes the President to European pressure. Wilson sails 4 December 1918. The American Commission is Wilson, Lansing, House, Henry White, and General Tasker H. Bliss.

Chapter III — General Plan for a League of Nations

Lines 1070–1762. Lansing holds that Wilson went to Paris chiefly to put a League into the treaty. From American entry in April 1917 Wilson treated a strong international agency as the condition of a lasting peace. Lansing supports a League in principle but opposes an affirmative military/economic guaranty of territory and independence, a primacy of the Great Powers, and haste in drafting. He prefers a “self-denying covenant” (negative undertaking not to violate independence and integrity) and equality of nations. The chapter includes the facsimile of Lansing’s commission as plenipotentiary.

Chapter IV — Substitute Articles Proposed

Lines 1763–2764. The President, White, and Lansing arrive in Paris 14 December 1918; House and Bliss are already there. On 23 December Lansing sends Wilson a “Secret and Urgent” letter from the Hôtel de Crillon warning that the proposed mutual guaranty will split other governments and raise constitutional, Monroe Doctrine, and congressional objections at home. He drafts substitute articles based on equality of nations and a self-denying rather than affirmative obligation. Wilson does not adopt them. This is the longest chapter and the core documentary brief on Lansing’s alternative Covenant language.

Chapter V — The Affirmative Guaranty and Balance of Power

Lines 2765–2868. Clemenceau’s Chamber speech of 30 December 1918 defends the balance of power and a concert of the four allied Great Powers. Wilson had just denounced the balance of power at Manchester. Lansing and House agree the doctrine must not be revived; Lansing argues that Wilson’s affirmative guaranty would in practice rest on the Great Powers and thus continue the same system under a new name.

Chapter VI — The President’s Plan and the Cecil Plan

Lines 2869–3237. Lansing examines Wilson’s draft Covenant (Appendix I). He finds loose drafting curable, but the use of force to make good the guaranty, Great-Power primacy, weak arbitration, no permanent international judiciary, and the mandatory system to be contrary to American ideals and traditions. He compares the draft with the published Smuts plan and with Lord Robert Cecil’s British plan (Appendix II), which he prefers on several points, including the absence of Wilson’s affirmative guaranty.

Chapter VII — Self-Determination

Lines 3238–3655. Tracks the mutual guaranty from Article III of Wilson’s original draft (territorial integrity plus future “self-determination” readjustments) through Article 7 of the revised draft to Article 10 of the Versailles Covenant. Lansing treats self-determination as a phrase dangerous in application: it invites irredentism, cannot be applied consistently (Ireland, India, American subject peoples), and was stripped from the final Article 10, which kept the guaranty without the safety-valve.

Chapter VIII — The Conference of January 10, 1919

Lines 3656–3748. At the Hôtel Crillon Wilson reviews his typewritten Covenant with the American Commissioners. Lansing raises objections of principle and policy; Wilson, as Lansing reads words and manner, will not receive them. Lansing then decides to take no further part in preparing or revising the Covenant text, lest subsequent silence be read as neglect or disloyalty. This episode is his explanation of later ignorance of League drafting.

Chapter IX — A Resolution Instead of the Covenant

Lines 3749–4154. After 10 January, Lansing tries delay: put only general principles in a preliminary treaty, then draft a detailed League later, preferably in a separate treaty or a world congress including neutrals. He prepares a resolution of declarations on the creation, nature, and purposes of a League. Wilson proceeds with a complete Covenant in the peace treaty.

Chapter X — The Guaranty in the Revised Covenant

Lines 4155–4279. In the three weeks before the League Commission meets, Wilson and House revise the draft with British and French statesmen; Gordon Auchincloss and David Hunter Miller produce a memorandum later published through Bullitt’s Senate hearing. The affirmative guaranty survives into the revised text.

Chapter XI — International Arbitration

Lines 4280–4531. On 3 February 1919, the day the League Commission first meets, Lansing writes Wilson from the Crillon on arbitral procedure: litigants’ national “judges” act as advocates; a permanent international court is needed. He treats this as a legal subject on which he may still speak without re-entering general Covenant drafting.

Chapter XII — Report of Commission on League of Nations

Lines 4532–5015. The Commission, Wilson presiding, holds ten meetings between 3 and 14 February 1919 and reports to a plenary of the Conference on the Preliminaries of Peace. Lansing praises Wilson’s presentation as literary oratory. The reported Covenant improves expression and drops some objectionable features of the first draft; if one accepts Wilson’s theory of organization, little remains to criticize except remaining inexactness. Lansing does not accept the theory.

Chapter XIII — The System of Mandates

Lines 5016–5431. Lansing objects to mandates on legal grounds and on policy: American guardianship of Near Eastern peoples aspiring to independence would break the tradition of aloofness from European political quarrels. The Council of Ten discusses mandates for German colonies and the Ottoman inheritance. Wilson rejects condominium and treats mandates as a duty of the new order. Lansing records the legal puzzle of sovereignty under a League mandate.

Chapter XIV — Differences as to the League Recapitulated

Lines 5432–5936. The differences are called irreconcilable. As a commissioned representative Lansing follows instructions rather than resign at Paris. He holds that Wilson endangered American and presidential prestige to obtain a defective League plan worked out with Smuts and Cecil, and that making the Covenant the principal feature of the German treaty was both wrong in principle and bad policy for Senate acceptance.

Chapter XV — The Proposed Treaty with France

Lines 5937–6345. Parallel American and British treaties of assistance to France in case of unprovoked German attack form, with France, a triple defensive alliance with the same object as Article 10 and even stronger affirmative force. Lansing’s 20 March note on “Impressions as to the Present Situation” records House circulating the proposal; White, Bliss, and Lansing agree that such an alliance would destroy the chief reason for the League as planned. Facsimile of Lansing’s full powers to negotiate the French treaty is among the plates.

Chapter XVI — Lack of an American Programme

Lines 6346–7128. A central charge: Wilson gave the Commissioners no detailed programme beyond the Fourteen Points and later addresses. The first five Points are principles; the next eight name territorial adjustments without boundaries; the Fourteenth is the League. Without authoritative interpretation the Commissioners lacked instructions “to negotiate peace.” Inquiry commissions and experts existed, but the President did not convert their work into a brief the delegation could use with other delegations.

Chapter XVII — Secret Diplomacy

Lines 7129–8104. Lansing attacks closed negotiations among the principal statesmen as a breach of “open covenants…openly arrived at.” Directing bodies—the Council of Ten (identical in membership with the Supreme War Council) and, from late March 1919, the Council of Four—never received a mandate from the full Conference. Wilson’s daily conversations with European leaders were seldom reported to the American Commission, except possibly to House. Smaller nations and even the American Commissioners were left in uncertainty.

Chapter XVIII — The Shantung Settlement

Lines 8105–8992. Near the last moment, with Orlando absent in Rome, Wilson, Clemenceau, and Lloyd George order Shantung articles granting former German rights in the peninsula to Japan. Japan, in return, drops a racial-equality amendment to the Covenant, agrees to sign the German treaty, and orally promises later restoration of Chinese sovereignty—a promise Lansing says was not confirmed in writing. He holds that an impartial commission would have decided otherwise, and that the American Commissioners considered resignation over the settlement.

Chapter XIX — The Bullitt Affair

Lines 8993–9311. William C. Bullitt, formerly of the American Commission, tells the Senate Foreign Relations Committee on 12 September 1919 that Lansing called many parts of the treaty thoroughly bad, especially Shantung and the League, and predicted the Senate would reject the treaty if it understood what it meant. The statement is published while Wilson is in the West appealing for the League. Lansing makes no comment on Bullitt’s disclosure of a confidential conversation; he includes it because Wilson later treated it as proof that Lansing’s mind had not gone along with his.

Conclusion

Lines 9312–9388. Recapitulation: from before Wilson’s 4 December 1918 departure through delivery of the German treaty on 7 May 1919, disagreements were many; Wilson rejected or ignored volunteered advice; Lansing followed unwillingly. Personal frictions were minor. The February 1920 letters are explained by these official differences (Wilson also cited Lansing’s calling Cabinet meetings during the President’s illness—mentioned as the cause Wilson specifically stated).

Appendices I–VI

Lines 9389–10750. I: Wilson’s original Covenant draft laid before the American Commission 10 January 1919. II: Cecil’s League plan. III: Covenant as in the Treaty of Versailles. IV: Fourteen Points (8 January 1918 address). V: Principles of 11 February 1918. VI: Versailles Articles 156–158 on Shantung (Kiaochow, Tsingtao–Tsinanfu Railway, cables to Shanghai and Chefoo).

Index

Lines 10751–11672. Useful entries: affirmative guaranty, American Commission, Article 10, Bliss, Bullitt, Cecil, Clemenceau, Council of Four/Ten, equality of nations, House, mandates, Monroe Doctrine, self-denying covenant, self-determination, Shantung, Wilson.

Coverage Guide

Use this source for the 1920 rupture and the Versailles inheritance: why Article 10 and the affirmative guaranty became the Senate’s target, how Shantung and the French alliance entered American postwar politics, and how Lansing narrated February 1920 as the delayed invoice of January–May 1919. It is not a military history of 1917–18 and not a substitute for the treaty text or the Senate record. Mine first the resignation letters, Bullitt/Senate coda, Article 10, French treaty, Shantung, and secret diplomacy; use paxson-recent-history-united-states and allen-only-yesterday for the public League fight and 1919–20 aftermath. Paris drafting mechanics remain primarily for the 1866–1919 sister page.

Notes on Scope

This is an interested party’s brief, published in 1921 to explain a forced resignation. Use it as direct evidence of Lansing’s positions, of documents he quotes, and of how the American Commission experienced Wilson’s method. Do not treat it as a complete or impartial history of the Conference or of Allied motives. The February 1920 Cabinet-meeting charge Wilson named in the correspondence is acknowledged in the Conclusion and not litigated in the body.
