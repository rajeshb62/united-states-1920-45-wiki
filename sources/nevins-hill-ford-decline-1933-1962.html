This is Volume III of the trilogy. Volume I ends near mass production and rising autocracy; Volume II ends with depression impact, V-8, and the symbol/legend/reality balance. This volume carries the firm through New Deal labor conflict, World War II, dynastic crisis, and managerial rebirth into the early 1960s.

Overview

The March 1963 preface (F.E.H.) frames an aging genius who fails to recover automotive supremacy and the tragedy of Edsel’s under-use; wartime chapters draw special help; picture sources include Ford Motor Company, UPI, and Wide World. The narrative contrasts Roosevelt’s experimental state with Henry Ford’s industrial individualism, then follows the company’s depression weakness relative to General Motors, the bitter labor decade culminating in the 1941 UAW contract, Bennett’s security empire, Willow Run and arsenal production, Edsel’s death and the succession struggle, Henry Ford II’s ouster of Bennett, Breech and the “Whiz Kids,” Ferguson litigation, automation and Korean War work, overseas postwar realignment, the Ford Foundation’s public stock sales, and a closing contrast between the 1896 shed and the 1950s Research and Engineering Center under shareholder and union constraints.

For this wiki the source is central for Ford in the New Deal and World War II, UAW organization in auto, Willow Run, Harry Bennett, Edsel’s last years, Henry Ford II, Ernest Breech, postwar corporate modernization, and the Ford Foundation as tax-and-philanthropy transformer of the family firm. It continues Expansion and Challenge and pairs with Since Yesterday, Now It Can Be Told (wartime production state), and As He Saw It (Roosevelt-era political frame).

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines ~200–259. Frank Ernest Hill at Columbia, March 1963. Frames the volume around the aging Henry Ford’s failure to regain supremacy and the tragedy of Edsel’s insufficient authority; notes wartime-chapter assistance; credits research associates Mira Wilkins, George B. Heliker, and William Greenleaf; picture acknowledgments (Ford, UPI, Wide World). Continues the Columbia–Ford Fund scholarly arrangement of the trilogy.

Chapter I — Crisis of 1933

Lines 598–1767. Noonday 4 March 1933: Roosevelt inaugurated on “bold, persistent experimentation” and fear itself; contrast with Henry Ford. Depression effects on the automotive industry and nation; the Big Three; Detroit banking crisis and new banks; NIRA/NRA and the automobile code. Ford refuses to sign; persuasion efforts and public reaction; Roosevelt’s attempts at a harmonious relationship. Flashback on Model A’s 1929 lead (1,710,734 units) and the industry’s climb from twenty-first to first in dollar value since 1909.

Chapter II — The Troubled March of Labor

Lines 1768–2921. NRA storm weathered; larger labor tempest gathers. Mid-depression plant conditions via Richard Frankensteen at Chrysler (49¢/hour; Works Council rebuff: “We don’t control the price of butter”). Workers take heart from New Deal and Section 7A; AFL under William Green of little help; Communist partial success. Battle at the Rouge gate and funerals for the dead; Briggs strike fails but pushes organization. Automotive code and Section 7A; Ford maintains independence. Threatened upheaval and Roosevelt settlement’s unsatisfactory features. Ford’s firm anti-union stand. NRA invalidated; CIO born; UAW joins; Bendix and Chrysler successes; Fisher Body next.

Chapter III — Challenge of the Future

Lines 2922–3858. Poor 1936 financial showing versus General Motors; Ford responsible through confused engineering, fetishes and taboos. Dealer and credit comparison across the Big Three; sales obstacles including former anti-Semitism, cigarette opposition, New Deal hostility. 1934 V-8: good car, advertising, Chicago World’s Fair exhibit; lower prices help; used-car market. Outside activities: Greenfield Village, soy beans, village industries. Partial success in 1937.

Chapter IV — Overseas Domain

Lines 3859–5229. Dagenham and European plans; Perry’s empery and Dearborn’s role; 1932 “baby Ford” essential and successful. Continental companies find Dagenham costs high; separate French and German manufacture. Mathis union produces Matford under Dollfus; Ford-France in 1936 strike; Mathis bought out; Dollfus defense-manufacturing plans. Weak then improving German Ford; Nazi control increases; Dearborn export help. Ford serves both sides in the Spanish Civil War. Hitler launches European war; world Ford companies add stability to the parent.

Chapter V — A Company in the Doldrums

Lines 5230–6283. J. R. Davis’s Used Car Week. Harry Bennett’s position, Ford Security force, increasing operational control, friends and supporters. Edsel’s role and Bennett’s attitude; Henry’s efforts to remake his son. Davis sales policies; Lincoln Zephyr and Mercury; New York World’s Fair exhibit. Davis–Harry Mack incident and Davis’s demotion; Doss becomes sales manager. Ford, Harry Ferguson, and the Ford-Ferguson tractor system. 1939 sales; improved national auto position; organization morale.

Chapter VI — A New Deal for Labor

Lines 6284–7899. GM–UAW conflict; sitdown brings GM surrender; Chrysler and U.S. Steel recognize unions. Ford and other employers still defiant; Bennett as Ford’s union agent; Edsel resentful. Determination to obstruct the Wagner Act. Battle of the Overpass: unfavorable publicity and firm NLRB opposition; cease-and-desist order; Ford appeals. Dallas and Kansas City strikes fail. UAW dissension: Homer Martin ousted, R. J. Thomas elected. Terror at the Rouge; Martin’s AFL dual union; April 1941 strike and plant election won by UAW. Ford signs a contract highly favorable to the union.

Chapter VII — Shadow of War

Lines 7900–9134. Ford belittles European war danger; liking for Germans; accepts decoration from German vice-consul. Oxford Group; relations with Lindbergh, who opposes American involvement after war starts. Defense preparations: Rolls-Royce engine contract accepted then cancelled; later Pratt & Whitney aircraft engines—plant and manufacturing improvements. Jeep overall design accepted. Lindbergh and Ford continue non-intervention stance. Liberator bomber contract via Sorensen; Willow Run planned; Ford versus Consolidated methods; M-4 tank and tank engine; M-7 aircraft director.

Chapter VIII — Arsenal of Democracy

Lines 9135–10543. Pearl Harbor; automotive industry’s defense preeminence as “high priests of production.” Edsel’s telegrams and 24-hour 7-day week; raised quotas for engines, M-7 directors, tanks. Aircraft-engine output; medium tank engines continue after tank production ceases; glider program. Willow Run as public symbol: construction difficulties, hangars, field; labor and transportation troubles; disappointing bomber pace; Truman Committee; work farmed out; production rises until Ford is chief bomber source. Summary of Ford war production.

Chapter IX — Power Is the Prize

Lines 10544–11569. Early 1944 press image of an empire with “acute indigestion”; Byzantine metaphor—aging sultan, vizier Sorensen versus janissary chief Bennett. Henry near eighty; Edsel’s ulcer operation and dubious health; succession vacuum. Intrigue and apprehension. Sorensen’s long service (“Sorensen period” 1925–1944); Bennett’s challenge; both rise by instant obedience to Henry. Henry’s conviction Edsel lacks steel; Sorensen later claims regent aims for Edsel’s heirs; Bennett wants power to keep. Labor-legend blame split; “Cast-Iron Charlie.” Bennett’s pay, appearance, followers; Ford’s further health decline; antagonism to Edsel’s sons. Edsel’s death; Henry assumes presidency; secret codicil to his will; situation precarious.

Chapter X — End of a Legend

Lines 11570–12471. Henry Ford II released from Navy (Secretary Knox, August 1943); Detroit bets he will share Edsel’s frustrations. Power triangle: sick Henry, clinging Sorensen, Bennett. Board meetings farcical when Henry attends (“Come on, Harry, let’s get the hell out of here”). Young Henry’s prior attention to production cards and plant news from the Navy. High officials discharged through Bennett; Sorensen’s difficult exit and past achievements. Henry II versus Bennett; Doss leaves; Bugas procured, Davis returned. Mysterious will destroyed. Mead Bricker takes Rouge charge for Henry II. Henry II becomes president and discharges Bennett. Henry Ford’s notable achievements recalled at the legend’s end.

Chapter XI — Outer Empire: War and Peace

Lines 12472–13379. Able chiefs: Perry (England), Albert (Germany), Dollfus (France); war divides control. Dollfus bows to Nazi invaders but wins independence from Schmidt of Ford-Werke over conquered territories. Schmidt and Albert protect properties manufacturing for Nazis; Dollfus serves Hitler independently. Camouflaged Dagenham: tractors, engines, carriers; Rolls-Royce Merlin and other war materials. French plants hit by Allied bombers; Cologne little injured. Americans arrive; postwar continental conditions; Ford of England in best shape.

Chapter XII — Reaching for Answers

Lines 13380–14380. Company condition September 1945; Henry II’s personality and persistent courage. Bennett followers purged; Policy Committee created. Postwar car production permitted but hedged; unresolved OPA conflict. Labor negotiation demands end of unauthorized strikes; early 1946 agreement. Search for a right-hand man; Thornton group (Whiz Kids) hired. Ernest R. Breech targeted; refuses while Bendix president; Ford persists; Breech finally: “My answer is Yes.”

Chapter XIII — To Rebuild a Company

Lines 14381–15704. Breech brings Lewis D. Crusoe (finance) and Harold T. Youngren (engineering); Delmar S. Harder sought for manufacturing; Albert J. Browning for purchasing. Crusoe’s GM/Fisher Body cost discipline; Youngren from Borg-Warner. Plans for new company, construction, curbing of old “drivers.” Disposal of money-losing properties (Brazilian rubber, soy factories, much of Ford Fleet). GM-style decentralized organization; Thornton group with Crusoe and Breech. Finances reformed. Henry Ford’s death. Foremen’s strike fails; improved foremen status planned. New UAW contract; Engineering Center plans; Dykstra plants. 1949 Ford shown; Ford Division created.

Chapter XIV — Lions in the Path

Lines 15705–16780. Ferguson’s 250millionsuitafterfailedcompromise;DearbornMotorsforFord’sowntractor;Ferguson’sdifficulties.Caseandcompanyreply.FordDivisionprogressunderCrusoe.NewfacilitiesatBuffalo,Cleveland,andelsewhere;moreassemblyplants.HarderandautomationadaptedtoFordfactories.KoreanWarproduction;quotasFordcallsunjust.Fergusonsuitsettled:DearbornMotorspays9,250,000; company profits and market share.

Chapter XV — Ford Becomes a Challenge

Lines 16781–17546. Fiftieth anniversary: Ford Archives; Engineering Center dedication. Henry II’s public activities. New plants; wider car range; Davis Committee. Power race among Chevrolet, Plymouth, and Ford; new divisions and personnel shifts. Tractors versus competitors. Reith’s program including the E-car (later Edsel)—fully approved.

Chapter XVI — Foreign Scene: The Postwar World

Lines 17547–18281. Henry II and Graeme K. Howard tour Europe; material, manpower, credit handicaps. English company sells European interests to Ford-US. Howard heads International Division; New York offices and large plans; Breech and Henry II curb him; Howard leaves but leaves a sound base. English prosperity; Sir Patrick Hennessy’s diesel; large UK construction. Ford-France difficulties; F. C. Reith restores efficiency; Ford-US sells French holdings to Simca. German Ford-Werke grows but Volkswagen leads. Canada and Australia. Rising volume and Ford-US ownership/control overseas.

Chapter XVII — The Great Transformer

Lines 18282–19194. Henry Ford’s individualism and rejection of organized charity; productive philanthropy and self-help; opposition to New Deal wealth-sharing; jobs and goods as true sharing. Personal aid, Greenfield Village, schools, Ford Hospital, Martha Berry. Ford Foundation created 15 January 1936 to avoid federal taxation of the Ford fortune. Through deaths of Edsel, Henry, and Clara, Foundation receives 95 percent of company stock (non-voting); 5 percent voting to Edsel’s children. 1948 program committee and Funds. By 1955, after ~$875 million granted or appropriated, sale with heirs of 3,600,000 shares to the public; further sales leave public stockholders with more than 60 percent of voting rights.

Chapter XVIII — Present and Future

Lines 19195–19871. Contrast: 1896 brick shed (Rockwell image of Henry and Clara with the quadricycle, now in Greenfield Village) versus 1956 Ford Research and Engineering Center campus—ten thousand white-shirted development staff, plant and equipment valued above $2.5 billion, ~180,000 average workers prior year. Stockholders’ meetings: 1903 twelve owners and tight control versus 1960 public meeting after share distribution—end of czarist non-meetings. Labor then and now: five-dollar day versus UAW negotiation. Laissez-faire original company versus social fabric of organized labor, stockholders, and government. 1955 labor settlement. Present character: size, growth, individuality; major loss on the Edsel; character of current Ford cars.

Notes, Appendices, and Index

Lines 19872–end. Chapter-keyed notes (I–XVIII). Appendix I: Ford Motor Company production report, 1903 through later years. Index runs to final pages (~507 book pages). Illustrations clusters document V-8s, Rotunda, Overpass, UAW contract signing, M-4, Willow Run, glider, Perry/Sorensen/Albert, Breech/Crusoe/McNamara “Team,” three Ford brothers, and 1950s–60s models.

Coverage Guide

New Deal, NRA, and depression auto politics

Chapters I–III place Ford against Roosevelt’s experimental state, the auto code refusal, Detroit banks, V-8 recovery efforts, and non-automotive side projects.

Labor organization and the UAW

Chapters II and VI are the core labor arc: Frankensteen exemplar, Rouge gate violence, Section 7A, CIO/UAW rise, Battle of the Overpass, NLRB, April 1941 strike and election, favorable union contract.

Bennett, Edsel, and succession

Chapters V, IX, and X cover Security force power, Edsel’s erosion, Sorensen–Bennett duel, Edsel’s death, secret will, Henry II’s return, and Bennett’s discharge.

World War II production

Chapters VII–VIII and XI cover non-intervention stigma, Pratt & Whitney, jeep, Willow Run Liberators, tanks, gliders, Truman Committee, and European Ford under occupation versus Dagenham’s Allied output.

Managerial rebirth

Chapters XII–XV cover Policy Committee, Whiz Kids, Breech–Crusoe–Youngren–Harder rebuild, GM decentralization model, 1949 Ford, Ferguson suit and automation, fiftieth anniversary and Edsel-car approval.

Foundation, public ownership, overseas

Chapters XVI–XVIII cover Howard’s International Division, Simca and Volkswagen contexts, Foundation stock and 1955 public sale, and the shed-to-Research-Center / autocrat-to-shareholder closing meditation.

Notes on Scope

Columbia-administered trilogy finale (© 1962/1963), archival and oral-history rich, carrying the firm past Henry Ford’s death into the managerial and shareholder order of the 1950s–early 1960s. Chronological center of gravity for this wiki is 1933–45 (NRA, UAW, Willow Run, succession crisis); postwar chapters remain valuable for completion of the Ford arc and for concepts (Foundation, public ownership, automation) that begin in the period. OCR requires caution on short verbatim quotes; chapter architecture and factual spine are reliable. Volume is complete as the third book, not as an endless corporate update beyond ~1962.
