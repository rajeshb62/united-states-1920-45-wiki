Overview

Allen frames the eleven years from the Armistice of 11 November 1918 to the stock-market panic culminating on 13 November 1929 as a distinct era—“the Post-war Decade”—whose destruction of Coolidge (and Hoover) Prosperity the crash hastened and dramatized. Writing in 1931 while recollection was still fresh, he deliberately emphasizes the changing state of the public mind, fads, fashions, and follies that touched daily life, and leaves much foreign policy to later historians. He acknowledges debt to Mark Sullivan’s method in Our Own Times and lists principal sources in an appendix rather than footnotes.

For this wiki the book is the natural 1920s narrative spine: demobilization and the League fight, the Big Red Scare, convalescence into mass amusements, the revolution in manners and morals, Harding and the oil scandals, Coolidge Prosperity and business culture, ballyhoo and Lindbergh, highbrow revolt, Prohibition and Capone, the Florida boom, the Big Bull Market, Crash, and the bitter 1930–31 aftermath. It pairs with Allen’s sequel Since Yesterday (1930s) once that source page is built, and later with Groves, Conant, Elliott Roosevelt, and the Nevins & Hill Ford volumes for wartime and industrial threads.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines 69–82. ~560 words.

This book is an attempt to tell, and in some measure to interpret, the story of what in the future may be considered a distinct era in American history: the eleven years between the end of the war with Germany (November 11, 1918) and the stock-market panic which culminated on November 13, 1929, hastening and dramatizing the destruction of what had been known as Coolidge (and Hoover) Prosperity. Writing so soon after the event means breaking new ground; many events of those years, Allen notes, have never been chronicled in full—including a continuous narrative of the Harding scandals and of the Big Bull Market as economic and social phenomenon. A contemporary history is bound to be anything but definitive, yet half the enjoyment of writing it has lain in reducing to order a mass of material untouched by previous historians. Emphasis on the public mind and on sometimes trivial preoccupations is deliberate: the close-range writer has a special chance to record fads, fashions, and follies while recollection is fresh, and may leave foreign affairs whose full effect is not yet measurable to subsequent historians. Allen credits Mark Sullivan’s Our Own Times for developing this method, and adds an appendix of principal sources in place of footnotes.

Chapter I — Prelude: May, 1919

Lines 83–146. ~4,560 words.

If time were suddenly to turn back to the earliest days of the Post-war Decade, what would seem strange? Allen follows a moderately well-to-do young couple—Mr. and Mrs. Smith of any large city—through an ordinary day in May 1919, six months after the Armistice, when war ways have largely given way to peace but the decade’s profound alterations have hardly begun. Men’s fashions move glacially; Mrs. Smith’s skirt hangs six inches from the ground, stockings are black or tan not flesh-colored, powder is possible but paint still frowned on by the well-brought-up, hair is long, and short hair still smells of radicalism. Vitamins are unknown; tabloids are not yet born (the New York Daily News will appear 26 June 1919). Front-page news is the Navy seaplane NC-4’s Azores route, the Peace Conference, Victory Loan, returning transports, strikes, Mayor Ole Hanson against the I.W.W., woman suffrage, and Henry Ford’s libel suit. Babe Ruth is still obscure on the Red Sox; Dempsey–Willard is set for Toledo; golf has not yet become the business man’s weekly ritual.

High Cost of Living dominates breakfast talk—milk, steak, butter, eggs, and rents far above 1914. Mr. Smith’s open car (only about a tenth of 1919 production closed) may be a Ford Model T cranked by hand; registered passenger cars are under seven million versus over twenty-three million a decade later; speed limits average about twenty miles an hour. At the office, business is looking up after post-Armistice uncertainty; labor demands and Bolshevism and stock speculation are the clouds. Radio broadcasting does not yet exist for the Smiths. Allen closes the prelude by listing the decade’s coming furniture of mind—Coué, Dayton, cross-word puzzles, Teapot Dome, Coral Gables, Sacco and Vanzetti, speakeasies, Capone, Lindbergh—and leaves the Post-war Decade lying before them.

Chapter II — Back to Normalcy

Lines 147–276. ~9,660 words.

Early on the morning of 11 November 1918, Woodrow Wilson writes in pencil on White House stationery that the armistice is signed, everything America fought for is accomplished, and it will now be the country’s duty to assist by example, sober counsel, and material aid in establishing just democracy throughout the world. Allen reads the message as Puritan schoolmaster, moral idealist, and dogmatic prophet of democracy—Wilsonian and almost Lincolnian—yet notes that Lincoln did not live to see what happens to idealism in a post-war decade. The 11th of November is carnival in the streets; then come demobilization, cancellation of war contracts, the slow return of the A.E.F., and the bitter fight over the Treaty and the League.

Lodge, the Italian vote and Fiume, the secret memorandum entrusted to Henry White for Balfour, Clemenceau, and Nitti, Borah and the irreconcilables, and Wilson’s western tour and collapse fill the political center. Harding emerges as the available man of “normalcy”; Cox and Roosevelt lose in 1920. Allen ends with Wilson in S Street on Armistice Day five years after the war—supported so he will not fall—declaring that fools who resist Providence will meet utter destruction, and dying three months later.

Chapter III — The Big Red Scare

Lines 277–436. ~9,630 words.

If Americans turned a deaf ear to Wilson’s League in the early Post-war Decade, it was not only weariness of foreign entanglements. They were listening to rumors of radical conspiracy, cocked for bombs and Bolshevist armies, millions of otherwise reasonable citizens thinking Red revolution might begin next month or next week. Front pages shout strikes and anti-Bolshevist riots; radicals shoot Armistice Day paraders in Centralia and a white I.W.W. member is lynched from a bridge; Socialist assemblymen are expelled in New York; an Indiana jury acquits a man for killing an alien who shouted “To hell with the United States.”

Palmer’s raids, the Fighting Quaker, deportations, super-patriotic societies, blacklists that touch even charities and theatres and colleges (Frankfurter, Chafee, Dewey, Main Street), race riot in Chicago, and the Wall Street explosion of 16 September 1920 carry the narrative. By the time the explosion’s mystery is still unsolved years later, Allen judges that the country was already coming to its senses enough to see that no such plot could command more than a handful of fanatics—had it come a few months earlier, the indirect consequences might have been uglier still.

Chapter IV — America Convalescent

Lines 437–498. ~3,390 words.

The Big Red Scare dies slowly: insufficient cause for the panic; Communism localized in Europe; radicalism ebbing at home; Palmer’s methods scaring many Reds into “pale pinkness”; A. F. of L. leaders leaning over backward toward conservatism; wets acquiring a belated concern for personal liberty. More important, the temper of war’s aftermath gives way to the temper of peace—like an overworked business man beginning vacation, the country learns again to relax and amuse itself.

Mah-jongg, radio, confession magazines, Main Street, The Sheik, If Winter Comes, and above all the Hall–Mills murder of 16 September 1922 near New Brunswick feed a national appetite for diversion and sensation. Sacco and Vanzetti run as a recurring political and emotional storm; when the headlines finally scream execution, men shiver, wonder whether justice or hideous mistake, and turn to where Lindbergh is flying and what General Motors is doing.

Chapter V — The Revolution in Manners and Morals

Lines 499–634. ~11,210 words.

A first-class revolt against the accepted American order is under way in the early Post-war Decade, but Lenin has nothing to do with it. Shock troops are sons and daughters of well-to-do families; defiance is spoken across the breakfast table. While elders still shiver at the Red Menace they awake to the Problem of the Younger Generation. The old code—woman as guardian of morality, innocent waiting for the altar, a double standard for men—cracks under war, prosperity, the automobile, prohibition’s hypocrisies, Freudian talk, and freer manners.

Skirts rise, bobbed hair moves from radical stigma (1918) to boom business contested by hairdressers and barbers, cosmetics and cigarettes spread, petting and companionate-marriage talk alarm the pulpits, and divorce statistics and sex in fiction and film mark the change. Middletown evidence and fashion economics run through the chapter. Not until the decade nears its end do signs appear that the revolutionists are learning to be at home in the freer code, less obsessed with sex, and to discover among the ruins of the old dispensation a new set of enduring satisfactions.

Chapter VI — Harding and the Scandals

Lines 635–856. ~11,260 words.

Allen opens with Harry M. Daugherty’s 1926 refusal to testify before a federal grand jury—personal attorney to the Hardings, Attorney-General, relations “of the most confidential character,” answers that might tend to incriminate. On 4 March 1921 Wilson limps from the White House; Harding is sworn in; the reign of normalcy begins. The Ohio Gang, Daugherty, Jess Smith, Forbes and the Veterans’ Bureau, Fall, Doheny, Sinclair, Teapot Dome and Elk Hills, the Continental Trading Company Liberty-bond packages to Blackmer, O’Neil, Stewart, and Sinclair, Senate investigation, and the slow unraveling after Harding’s death in 1923 fill the narrative.

Coolidge inherits the mess; prosecutions and political embarrassment drag through the decade. When Hoover and Coolidge finally take part in dedicating Harding’s tomb in June 1931, Allen notes a certain restraint—it is not so easy as in 1923 to compose panegyrics on the good-natured man who had “taught us the power of brotherliness.”

Chapter VII — Coolidge Prosperity

Lines 857–987. ~8,520 words.

Business is booming when Harding dies; by kerosene lamp in a Vermont farmhouse Colonel John Coolidge administers the presidential oath to Calvin. The depression of 1921 has given way to improvement in 1922 and rushing revival in 1923. Stock prices on 2 August 1923 still suggest no unreasonable optimism—Steel 87, small volume—and the Big Bull Market is still far ahead, but the tide of prosperity is in full flood. Allen traces the jagged statistical path of the decade to the Everest of 1929 and the abyss of 1930–31.

Mass production, installment buying, the automobile and good roads, electrification, chain stores, advertising’s rise from business to self-styled profession to, among practitioners, a “racket,” service clubs, Rotary and boosters, the fusion of religion and business (The Man Nobody Knows), and Coolidge’s discreet obeisance before the great god business structure the chapter. Statesmanship of a sort Coolidge represents: encourage prosperity without hot water; accept the inevitable; do not try to be bolder than circumstances safely permit.

Chapter VIII — The Ballyhoo Years

Lines 988–1219. ~12,380 words.

One striking mark of Coolidge Prosperity is the unparalleled rapidity and unanimity with which millions fix attention on tremendous trifles—a heavyweight fight, a murder trial, a new car model, a transatlantic flight. Most causes célèbres are unimportant by traditional historical measure; that they could engage unprecedented numbers is not. Allen reviews Hall–Mills and the “pig woman,” Floyd Collins in the Kentucky cave, sports idols, beauty contests, cross-word puzzles, radio crazes, and above all Charles A. Lindbergh’s flight as the heroism of a stunt flyer who crossed the ocean for a money prize.

The Dayton Scopes trial is told at length: Bryan on the stand before reason as prosecutor, Raulston’s mercy, Scopes guilty and fined one hundred dollars, the law upheld on technicality but Fundamentalism losing in civilized opinion as the slow drift from certainty continues. By early 1928 ballyhoo’s bloom may be departing—Lindbergh has spoiled the public for lesser heroes; Sacco–Vanzetti and the 1928 campaign remind a well-fed people of public issues; and a ten-point gain in Radio common in a single day promises more immediate benefits than all the non-stop flyers and heavyweight champions in the world.

Chapter IX — The Revolt of the Highbrows

Lines 1220–1309. ~5,830 words.

Allen opens with Fitzgerald’s generation that found all Gods dead, all wars fought, all faiths in man shaken. Wartime social compulsion becomes peacetime habit—drives, quotas, canned publicity, the man who grips his pocketbook feeling mass opinion. Intellectuals revolt: against puritanism and censorship, against the exaltation of the business man, against the reformer (by 1925 a term of contempt among them), often in religious skepticism, united in scorn of the bourgeois majority held responsible for prohibition, Fundamentalism, and other repressions—Babbitts, Rotarians, the Klan, Service-with-a-Smile, boosters and super-salesmen, with Mencken as tone-setter.

Disillusionment and the lost generation’s meager pleasure in self-pity cannot find peace; Allen closes on groping for new values among those for whom the new day heralded in wartime had turned to night before it arrived.

Chapter X — Alcohol and Al Capone

Lines 1310–1431. ~7,770 words.

Had one told the average American in 1919 that prohibition would be the most violently explosive public issue of the nineteen-twenties, he would have thought the prophecy mad. Rum-ships outside the twelve-mile limit, hijacked beer trucks, stills by the carload, fashionable contraband cocktails, speakeasy grills, and Capone in an armor-plated car would have dropped his jaw. The Eighteenth Amendment, ratified to take effect 16 January 1920, had seemed to settle the issue.

Allen traces the ease of final dry victory after generations of uphill fighting, then the collapse of enforcement, wet and dry geography, corruption, Chicago gang war, Capone’s rise, rackets beyond liquor, and the Wickersham Commission’s paradoxical 1931 report—sorry inability to dry the country, individual commissioners split, corporate vote for further trial—satirized in Flaccus’s summary in F.P.A.’s column. As the decade bows out, prohibition, gangster, and racket problems remain unsolved for the nineteen-thirties; still the rum-running launch slips across the river and tabloid-readers find adventure in gangster killings.

Chapter XI — Home, Sweet Florida

Lines 1432–1547. ~5,960 words.

Allen quotes a bank vice-president’s unbuttoned 1925 rhetoric in the Miamian—palms, sun-kissed sky, Caribbean breeze—as the Florida boom hits its height amid Scopes aftermath, Red Grange, and The Man Nobody Knows. Miami becomes one frenzied real-estate exchange: thousands of offices and agents, binder boys, Coral Gables and the American tropics as sales religion, lot prices in a fever unmatched in ninety years.

The mayors’ “Fiesta of the American Tropics” at the turn of 1925–26 promises Love, Good Fellowship, and Sublime Beauty; by New Year’s the suspicion insinuates itself that new buyers are not appearing as they should. Collapse, bank strain, and the shift of speculative infection from Flagler Street to Broad and Wall follow. Florida house-lots a bad bet? Then, asks a public still enthralled by Coolidge Prosperity, what else is there to bet on? The Big Bull Market is getting under way.

Chapter XII — The Big Bull Market

Lines 1548–1738. ~9,540 words.

One day in February 1928 an investor asks an astute banker about common stocks; the banker thinks them dangerously high and would wait. By conservative canons he is right: Coolidge Prosperity confidence has already lifted prices far since 1924. Through 1927 speculation increases; brokers’ loans rise from about 2.82billionto3.56 billion; a record week of trading hits in December. After-dinner talk from New York to the lowliest village runs to the market.

Allen follows the 1928–29 advance: Radio and the new-era argument, investment trusts, rejection of “ten times earnings,” breaks in June and December 1928 that teach the lesson “when there is a break it is a good time to buy,” call money at 8 and 9 percent explained away, and the vision of an America set free from poverty and toil—cars, airplanes, high-tension wires, skyscrapers, and smartly dressed men and women spending with money won by being far-sighted enough, way back in 1929, to foresee what was going to happen.

Chapter XIII — Crash!

Lines 1739–1855. ~6,050 words.

Early in September the stock market breaks, recovers to a Times averages high on 19 September even above 3 September, then slips farther; by 4 October Steel, Can, General Electric, and Radio look like bargains to those trained by earlier breaks. Brokers’ loans still mount to a new high about $6.8 billion on 2 October—margin buyers undiminished. Then comes the cascade of mid-October and the panic days: 23 October’s Niagara of liquidation; Thursday the 24th’s forced selling as margins exhaust, the ticker lagging, the roar of panic on the floor; the midday bankers’ pool at Morgan’s—Mitchell, Wiggin, Potter, Prosser, Lamont, Baker—forty millions apiece to keep trading orderly, not to peg prices.

Black Tuesday and the weeks that follow shatter the price structure. Allen’s close: the Big Bull Market had been more than the climax of a business cycle; it had been the climax of a cycle in American mass thinking and mass emotion. With it gone and prosperity going, Americans face an altered world. The psychological climate is changing. The Post-war Decade has come to its close. An era has ended.

Chapter XIV — Aftermath: 1930–31

Lines 1856–1949. ~5,780 words.

Not without long and unhappy protest does the country accept the breakdown of Coolidge–Hoover Prosperity—bitter for a Republican party that had half forgotten the business cycle’s independence of political policies, bitterest for Hoover who had spoken of abolishing poverty. After the market goes over Niagara, the country turns to the President. He promises tax reduction, conferences against wage cuts, public works, and a campaign of reassurance that “conditions” are “fundamentally sound.” Mellon, Lamont, Barnes, and Hoover set dates for recovery; a Little Bull Market in early 1930 regains much ground, then sickens in April as the Coué formula fails against organic disease.

Allen lists diseases: overproduction of capital and goods; artificial commodity prices and pools; silver’s collapse; gold shifts to France and the United States; foreign unrest; the self-generating effect of depression; and the profound psychological reaction from 1929’s exuberance. Wheat crashes despite the Farm Board; industrial production sinks; banks fail including the Bank of the United States in New York; unemployment approaches some six million; apple salesmen stand on corners. Fashion and manners show armistice in the moral revolution—freedom kept, excited sense of smashing taboos gone. Allen ends: the stream of time often doubles on its course, but always makes for itself a new channel.

Appendix — Sources and Obligations

Lines 1950–1991. ~1,410 words.

Allen records principal debts: the Lynds’ Middletown; the Beards’ Rise of American Civilization (including the Washington Conference); William Allen White on Wilson, Harding, and Coolidge; Charles Merz on prohibition and related books; World Almanac, New York Times Index, and Times files. Chapter notes point to Baker on Wilson and the Peace Conference, Chafee on free speech and the Palmer raids, Nystrom on fashion, Lippmann and Krutch on morals and disillusion, Ravage and Bliven on oil and the Ohio Gang, Stuart Chase on prosperity, Silas Bent on ballyhoo, Pasley on Capone, Vanderblue on Florida, Whitney on the Exchange in the panic, and James Truslow Adams on “Presidential Prosperity.” Personal thanks close with Dorothy Penrose Allen. Dated Scarsdale, New York, June 1931.

Coverage Guide

Mine first as the period’s popular-contemporary spine for 1919–1931. Chapters I–IV support demobilization, League and normalcy politics, Red Scare, and early-1920s cultural convalescence. Chapters V–X are strongest for manners, Harding scandals, Coolidge business culture, ballyhoo, intellectuals, and Prohibition/crime. Chapters XI–XIV are the core speculative cycle: Florida, Bull Market, Crash, and Hoover-era aftermath into 1931. Use the Appendix as a contemporary bibliography map, not as later historiography.

Notes on Scope

Near-contemporary informal history (composed by June 1931), not archival monograph. Exceptionally strong on atmosphere, sequence of public crazes, and the speculative psychology of 1925–29; individual scandal and market details should be cross-checked against specialized sources when building detailed entity or event pages. Foreign affairs are intentionally thin by Allen’s own design.
