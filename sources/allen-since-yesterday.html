Overview

Allen’s sequel to Only Yesterday chronicles the decade from the peak of the Big Bull Market on 3 September 1929 to the British and French declarations of war on Germany on 3 September 1939—ten years to the day. He began serious work late in 1938, had the manuscript three-quarters done by late summer 1939 without knowing how the story would end, and took the European war as the natural close of an era, as the Panic of 1929 had closed the twenties. Overlap with Only Yesterday is deliberate: Chapter I revisits the crest day; Chapter II covers Panic through spring 1931 in abbreviated form so the thirties volume can show the height from which the country fell. Emphasis shifts from twenties fads and follies toward “the enormous economic and political transformation” of Depression and New Deal, though manners, radio, movies, sports, and popular culture remain thickly present.

For this wiki the book is the natural 1930s narrative spine: exit from prosperity, black depression, the Hoover–Roosevelt transfer and bank crisis, New Deal honeymoon and reform battles, Dust Bowl and rural change, 1936 landslide and Roosevelt hatred, cultural left and Hollywood, sit-downs and Court fight and Recession, and the shadow of European war. It pairs with Only Yesterday for 1919–31 atmosphere, and later with Groves, Conant, Elliott Roosevelt, and the Nevins & Hill Ford volumes for wartime science, diplomacy, and industry.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines 223–232. ~510 words.

Ever since, in Only Yesterday, Allen tried to tell the story of life in the United States during the nineteen-twenties, he had it in mind to attempt something similar for the thirties. He began work late in 1938 and had the book three-quarters done by late summer 1939 without knowing how the story would end. The outbreak of war in Europe provided an obvious conclusion, ending an era as the Panic of 1929 had ended one. By an odd chance, the British and French declarations of war upon Germany fell ten years to the day after 3 September 1929, already chosen as the first chapter’s subject. Only Yesterday had run from the Armistice of 11 November 1918 to the Panic of October–November 1929, with a concluding chapter into spring 1931 (published December 1931). Some overlapping is necessary so the thirties story can start before the Panic and show the high place from which the country fell in 1929–32; the Panic itself is abbreviated because it was told at length in the earlier book. Selection and emphasis are harder close to the event: the heart of the thirties story is economic and political transformation, and the trivialities essential to the twenties are less significant here. Future events may date his appraisal; he hopes even miscalculations may show what pitfalls one readily fell into in 1939.

Chapter I — Prelude: September 3, 1929

Lines 233–346. ~6,540 words.

Do you remember what you were doing on September 3, 1929? Allen refreshes the recollection by re-living a single day when the Dow-Jones average made its all-time high and the wave of prosperity and speculation of the twenties attained its utmost height before it curled over and crashed. It is a very hot day from Maine to Nebraska. He walks the reader through streets, offices, brokers’ board rooms, newspapers, radio and talkies, sports, and household assumptions—margin speculation by well over a million people, brokers’ loans over eight billion dollars, call money at nine percent, Steel edging toward 261¾, Radio on a split basis looking absurdly high to later eyes but not to most men in the room.

A gallery of “a few people, 1929” and the decade’s furniture of mind closes the prelude: Shirley Temple is a baby less than five months old; the Dionne quintuplets are unborn; and so, for that matter, is the Depression. To be set down as the craziest of prophets one need only tell these men and women that within two months they will witness the greatest financial panic in American history and a prolonged economic crisis.

Chapter II — Exit Prosperity

Lines 347–484. ~6,990 words.

After September 3 the market drops, surges, drops again and does not surge back. At first there is little uneasiness—earlier breaks had been buying opportunities—and brokers’ loans rise to a final peak over eight and a half billion while prognosticators insist nothing is amiss. Foreign funds withdraw after Hatry’s collapse in England; industrial production slips. Then comes the October cascade and panic (abbreviated relative to Only Yesterday), followed by the afterglow of 1930: Hoover’s conferences and public works and “fundamentally sound” assurances, a Little Bull Market, then sickening relapse.

Bathtub gin, crime wave and racketeers, miniature golf and free wheeling, Wickersham’s confused Prohibition report, and Hoover’s political trouble fill the social middle. Allen’s close: traditional economic laws no longer seem to work; the learned are as baffled as anyone; Russia offers an alternative few embrace; the world enters bewilderment, mutual suspicion, and readiness for desperate measures—and the United States, falling from such a pinnacle, will not escape the confusion and dismay of readjustment.

Chapter III — Down, Down, Down

Lines 485–720. ~9,870 words.

June 1931: twenty months after the Panic. Eugenie hats, Wiley Post and Gatty’s Winnie Mae, Starr Faithfull, Cornell in The Barretts of Wimpole Street, The Green Pastures, Pearl Buck’s The Good Earth, and the fading twenties sports heroes set the cultural surface while the economy sinks. The Hoover Moratorium, the RFC as “a dole for corporations,” “Oh, Yeah?” skepticism, black depression statistics, freight-car hoppers too numerous for railroad police, servants discharged, golf clubs resigned, lunch counters instead of clubs—Allen inventories the descent.

The Lindbergh kidnap case becomes the decade’s absorbing crime drama. Stock tables from September 1929 highs through November 1929 panic lows to 1932 bottoms (Telephone, GE, Motors, Central, Radio, Steel) close the chapter: thus spoke the sensitive barometer; thus had departed the hopes of yesteryear. Was there no savior anywhere in sight?

Chapter IV — A Change of Government

Lines 721–882. ~8,120 words.

It begins to look as if saving the United States will fall into Franklin D. Roosevelt’s willing hands. The Republicans’ dull June 1932 convention renominates Hoover because it must. Democrats at Chicago—still reeling from a local bank panic—see Roosevelt’s long lead managed by Jim Farley (green-ink mass correspondence, six stenographers) and Louis Howe (asthmatic chief-of-staff on a Madison Avenue day bed). Nomination, the flight to Chicago, the platform, and the campaign against a doomed Hoover follow.

Bonus Army, rebellion and ferment, Technocracy’s brief national craze (Scott, Veblen, Soddy, energy survey, then December 1932 saturation), and the final banking collapse bring the curtain down. On the morning of 4 March 1933 a worn Hoover is told the banking system has stopped: “We are at the end of our string… There is nothing more we can do.” Allen’s judgment: for all Hoover’s asperities and political ineptitudes he had been a resourceful soldier of a doomed order and deserved no such personal humiliation—but the curtain was coming down.

Chapter V — New Deal Honeymoon

Lines 883–1064. ~8,050 words.

Saturday, 4 March 1933. Turn on the radio. Banks are shut; people read typewritten notices and walk away wondering how far five or ten dollars will go. The inaugural—“the only thing we have to fear is fear itself”—is what a huddled country wants to hear. Allen narrates the rush: bank holiday and Emergency Banking Act, fireside chat, beer and the road to Repeal, CCC, AAA, TVA, securities regulation, abandonment of gold, NRA and the Blue Eagle, public works, and the President’s intent to raise prices so debtors repay in dollars of the kind they borrowed (later progressive devaluation toward 59.06 cents gold).

Spring 1933 brings a shot-in-the-arm jump in prices, inventories, stocks, and the Reserve Board’s production index. All roads lead to Washington; the Hundred Days feel like multiple deals at once. Though the market looks ragged by late summer and indices slip from July’s peak, the prevailing public mood matches Disney’s three little pigs: America has learned to sing “Who’s Afraid of the Big Bad Wolf?”

Chapter VI — A Change of Climate

Lines 1065–1234. ~10,170 words.

Social change is continuous, yet the climate alters. Most contrasts between twenties and thirties manners do not become clearly marked until about 1933—New Deal and Repeal—as if Americans had walked backward into Depression holding twenties customs, then turned face-forward into the new decade. The post-war revolution in manners (knee-length skirts, hip flasks, speakeasies, petting, Freudian talk, anti-Puritan defiance) cools into something less frantic; marriage and morals, fashion’s longer line, Repeal and the persistence of bootleg stills and rum-runners, sports and gambling (bingo, slot machines, Bank Night, softball), churches, and social-salvationist politics structure the chapter.

Fortune and revenue data suggest most Americans are glad Prohibition ended even while illicit liquor remains large. Underneath runs fear: old-age pensions, social security, the wish for rock-firm certainty. Except during the brief honeymoon when hope rode high, an undercurrent of fear persists—they want security under their feet and do not have it.

Chapter VII — Reform—and Recovery?

Lines 1235–1442. ~10,320 words.

The honeymoon ends in late 1933 not abruptly but in annoyances and discords. Industrial production’s spring leap from 59 to 100 collapses by November to 72—two-thirds of the gain lost while NRA accumulates momentum. Ford refuses the auto code; Hearst brands NRA “No Recovery Allowed.” Codes creak; price-fixing and little-business complaints multiply. Relief expands: CWA, then WPA with theatres, orchestras, murals; transient camps; NYA; surplus commodities; PWA and CCC as related giant works.

Dillinger, G-Men, and Dewey supply crime melodrama; Huey Long, Coughlin, Townsend, and other voices of discontent press from the flanks. The Supreme Court begins saying no to central New Deal statutes. Allen closes on the President’s long-horizon talk of what the people must decide in five or ten years—without yet a concrete path beyond eventual constitutional reckoning.

Chapter VIII — When the Farms Blew Away

Lines 1443–1542. ~5,770 words.

Armistice Day 1933: the first great dust storm sweeps South Dakota. Allen quotes R. D. Lusk’s Saturday Evening Post account of the Karnstrum farm in Beadle County—opaque black noon, handkerchiefs over faces, coughing black, fences and machinery buried—as type for storms from the Texas Panhandle to the Canadian border over the next two years. Black blizzards, ruined grasslands, and migrant tracks structure the disaster narrative.

Tenant mobility, “suitcase farmers,” tractors and larger-scale commercial farming, floods and dams, and the sense of a mature America that can no longer plunder and move on close the chapter. Durable development west of the Plains will require more disciplined pioneering than the old—even while the dispossessed still wander homeless through the land.

Chapter IX — The Voice with the Smile Wins

Lines 1543–1717. ~10,790 words.

Winter 1935–36 atmosphere: “The Music Goes ’Round and ’Round,” Major Bowes, Astaire and Rogers, Mr. Deeds, Shirley Temple, Life with Father, best sellers from It Can’t Happen Here toward Santayana’s The Last Puritan. Allen maps the changed world of 1936: pump-priming that works up to a point, streamlined trains and trailers, and the hatred of Roosevelt among eastern business and commuting classes—Mark Sullivan, club-car stories, “delusions of grandeur—He thinks He’s Franklin D. Roosevelt”—most intense on the Atlantic seaboard until exhaustion softens it around 1938.

Landon as “the Kansas Coolidge,” the campaign, and the landslide (“the voice with the smile wins”) return Roosevelt with something like a blank check. A coda on Edward VIII’s abdication broadcast lets Americans turn back to what their own chief of state will write upon that check.

Chapter X — With Pen and Camera Through Darkest America

Lines 1718–1897. ~9,450 words.

A 1925 New York cocktail party of writers and artists would have screamed over Martinis for sex freedom, fewer laws, scorn of Babbitts and business, debunking of Victorian heroes, and preference for Europe. A similar 1935 gathering has inverted the conversation: social salvation, economic justice, and American materials dominate; Queen Victoria and Longfellow return to favor via Hayes and Van Wyck Brooks. Tobacco Road, best sellers, proletarian and fellow-traveler writing, the Communist appeal and its limits, candid-camera documentary impulse, Benny Goodman and swing’s jitterbug vocabulary beside renewed Bach, censorship fights (“You Can’t Say That”), and Hollywood’s heaven of glamour and box office fill the tour.

Allen’s lesson from eighty-five million movie admissions: America is not—or not yet—proletarian-minded. Citizens will organize and use government to redress wrongs, yet still keep room for an Horatio Alger paradise where valour and glamour rise and live happily ever after.

Chapter XI — Friction and Recession

Lines 1898–2098. ~10,060 words.

In cold rain before the Capitol, Roosevelt takes the oath for a second term and speaks of one-third of a nation ill-housed, ill-clad, ill-nourished—the test of progress whether enough is provided for those who have too little. Listeners wait for something more specific. Sit-downs and the CIO, Taylor, Lewis, and Girdler, the Supreme Court battle (Wheeler’s Democratic opposition, Hughes’s letter, the Court’s spring 1937 prudence upholding railway labor, farm mortgage, minimum wages, Wagner Act), 1937 montage, and the sharp Recession follow.

By late 1937–38 prestige in Congress is weakened, economic policies look wanting, unemployment remains hateful. Was the New Deal played out? Perhaps—but if so the fact is being obscured by storm clouds rolling in from Europe that will make citizens look on country and government with new eyes.

Chapter XII — The Shadow of War

Lines 2099–2272. ~10,110 words.

Studio Nine: H. V. Kaltenborn at a CBS microphone through the September 1938 Czech crisis, millions listening while strapless evening dresses and local politics pause. “We take you now to Prague” becomes the sound of a world closing in. Isolation versus intervention, neutrality legislation, arms embargo debates, the Martian panic of Welles’s War of the Worlds beside real German advances, New York World’s Fair’s “World of Tomorrow,” a royal visit, and a summer lull structure the approach.

Liberals who once laughed at red scares now fear Nazi propaganda and German–Latin American trade; dinner tables talk of planes from South American bases and the Panama Canal. Then Chamberlain’s tired radio voice from London: no German undertaking to withdraw from Poland—this country is at war with Germany. With those sentences, spoken so quietly thousands of miles away, an era ends for America and another begins.

Appendix — Sources and Obligations

Lines 2273–2314. ~2,420 words.

Allen again places the Lynds first—Middletown in Transition (1937) as indispensable as Middletown had been for the twenties. For Hoover chapters: Myers and Newton’s documented narrative; Joslin’s Hoover Off the Record. For the New Deal: Roosevelt’s Public Papers and Addresses; the Beards’ America in Midpassage; Moley’s After Seven Years. Chapter notes point to Flynn and Mitchell on NRA and Johnson, Bromley and Britten and Fortune on youth, Harrison and Laine after Repeal, Lubell on nickels and slots, and many magazine pieces on sports, crime, and culture. Personal thanks close with Agnes Rogers Allen. Dated New York City, 10 November 1939.

Coverage Guide

Mine first as the period’s popular-contemporary spine for 1929–1939. Chapters I–III support the crash-to-trough economic and social descent and Hoover-era atmosphere. Chapters IV–V are strongest for the 1932–33 transfer, bank crisis, and Hundred Days. Chapters VI–X carry manners, reform battles, Dust Bowl, 1936 politics, and culture. Chapters XI–XII are core for labor/Court/Recession and the approach of war through 3 September 1939. Use the Appendix as a contemporary bibliography map. Panic mechanics remain fuller in Only Yesterday Chapter XIII.

Notes on Scope

Near-contemporary informal history (preface dated 10 November 1939), not archival monograph. Exceptionally strong on sequence of public mood from crash through New Deal to war’s edge; individual statute, market, and diplomatic details should be cross-checked against specialized sources when building detailed entity or event pages. Foreign affairs thicken only in the final chapter by design; the European crisis is seen primarily as it registers on American radio, opinion, and neutrality politics.
