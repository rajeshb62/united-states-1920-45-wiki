Overview

Writing in 1923 amid the postwar agricultural and industrial deflation, Lindbergh offers a populist–progressive indictment of “profiteers” as a fourth, useless group above farmers, wage workers, and legitimate business. Book I diagnoses concentration of wealth, capital–labor conflict, kept press and Congress, the Federal Reserve Act as a private profit system, “scientifically created” panics, the war boom and 1920–22 deflation, the Esch-Cummins railway guarantee spirit, congressional investigations and rules, legislative blocs, and “progressive” inadequacy. Book II shifts to program: business and politics as a game whose rules must be rewritten by united farmer–labor–useful-business federations, team-work, American associations on common ground, world relations, and government “by the people—for the people.”

For this wiki the tract is a near-contemporary primary voice of Midwestern agrarian–anti-monopoly politics in the early Coolidge years—Federal Reserve and railway legislation, Liberty Bond transfers, farm distress, and bloc politics—set against Allen’s more middle-class Only Yesterday narrative of the same decade.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter (title, copyright, contents)

File pages 7–12. Title The Economic Pinch by C. A. Lindbergh; Dorrance, Philadelphia; copyright 1923. Contents list Book I chapters I–XXII (printed pp. 9–191) and Book II chapters I–V (printed pp. 199–236) plus General Index (247).

Book I — Chapter I — A Key to the Good Things

File pages 15–22. ~1,570 words.

The key to the world’s good things is the mind; failure of honest thought leaves most people under the influence of a few who control the things that control others. Lindbergh sketches farmer, wage worker, and small business man as groups whose futures are gambles, then names a fourth group that has “taken possession.” Politics still fails to give the public satisfaction because office-holding, not condition-bettering, is most politicians’ object. Protest votes and rosy candidates do not yield industrial independence; business interests must first be understood in relation to each other before political agreement is possible.

Book I — Chapter II — Three Useful Groups; One Useless Group

File pages 23–26. ~720 words.

Economically the nation divides into three useful groups—farmers, wage workers, and business managing exchange and distribution—plus a fourth, the profiteers, not classed as useful though some individuals do useful work. The three useful groups have been half antagonistic, especially politically, and lose by it. They should establish a common field and conference committees, then present united concrete demands so officials of any party cannot dodge. The United States Chamber of Commerce is cited as a private model of how interests put measures in form and force action; profiteers draft bills whose titles promise relief while the body takes it away. Existing conditions are intolerable and leading to chaos.

Book I — Chapter III — The American Nobility

File pages 27–30. ~705 words.

European kings could not make the nobility bear taxes, so burdens fell on peasants and produced revolutions. American profiteers ape that nobility but are better fortified: they shift taxes and burdens, rob by authority of law, and secure trust-made statutes through subservient officials. Whichever party the people elect, officials betray them; exceptions are never enough. The country nears a state of anarchy; the super-wealthy drive toward destruction; hope lies in popular action that may even save the profiteers from their own race for stolen wealth—not as profiteers.

Book I — Chapter IV — The Capital and Labor Conflict

File pages 31–41. ~2,010 words.

Government and business administration have created capital–labor conflict that harms farmer, wage worker, and useful business and will wreck the capitalist too if continued. Capital presently has the best of it. Workers do not deny property rights but deny owners’ right to use property to subject industry to toll. War and postwar inflation made thousands of new millionaires; then farmers were pressed to expand and sell below cost while wage workers were deflated by idleness and wage cuts. Centralized wealth bought Liberty Bonds and other assets at discount and became a compounding liability charged against producing energy. When capital “strikes,” the press calls it hard times or panic; when labor strikes, labor is condemned. Railway Labor Board language equating labor’s lot with capital’s “earnings” (profits) is read as the keynote of official bias. Machinery’s gains partly delay the penalty but also swell capital that workers must again pay profits upon.

Book I — Chapter V — The Capitalistic Aim

File pages 42–50. ~1,820 words.

Railroad capital already approximates twenty billions including water; with other trusts and wealth controllers the total approaches or reaches two hundred billions, whose compounded profits under present law will take all workers’ earnings above bare subsistence. Capital will not destroy the worker any more than a farmer destroys his team, but will make labor the slave on bare keep. Minimum wage and minimum farm price are not fundamental remedies though temporarily favor-able; the fallacy of legal “reasonable profit” is the main trouble and a seed of future wars. Lindbergh does not condemn all profit—human beings still need inducement—but insists much better rules are already within reach.

Book I — Chapter VI — Classification of Property Relations

File pages 51–59. ~1,310 words.

Exaggerated profit to the few unsettles the world and amounts to industrial slave service. Readjustment of business relations to present intelligence is required. A small percentage group can double wealth by cutting farm prices and wages, raising living costs, or forcing more hours and output. Permanent peace is impossible while labor is forced to guarantee profit as under Esch-Cummins practice and Federal Reserve sanction. Strikes are usually rebellions against wrong conditions; correction points to Washington, where important laws are made.

Book I — Chapter VII — The Press, and Others

File pages 60–71. ~2,375 words.

A sample capitalist-paper editorial on the railroad shopmen’s strike and the Railway Labor Board’s outlawing of the organization illustrates kept-press framing. Unlike the Revolutionary generation, the people have means of correction short of force, yet they still petition while measures are pigeonholed or worsened. Profiteers exploit through the Federal Reserve Bank Act and Esch-Cummins Railway Act and through gifts of water-powers, minerals, and forests; the bank and railway acts are the greatest public gifts to wealth.

Book I — Chapter VIII — Believing + Voting — Thinking

File pages 72–82. ~2,135 words.

National catastrophe follows from accepting kept-press falsehoods, adroit schemes of the wealthy, and Congressmen who sacrifice constituents for press support and committee place. The Federal Reserve Act’s title implies public good and government ownership; Lindbergh calls that promise a deliberate lie—the government owns no stock; “IT IS A SNAKE.” Subsidiary chambers of commerce and dominated associations secure what wealth demands. Note on W. P. G. Harding of the Federal Reserve Board and missions such as Cuban financial reorganization during inflation and early deflation years.

Book I — Chapter IX — Banks and the People

File pages 83–88. ~1,150 words.

Everyone is interested in banks; bankers direct the course of business more than the press molds opinion. Bigger is not always stronger; borrowers determine standing. Finance in big bankers’ hands practically controls business interests; Europe in 1923 is stranded on finance. Banks are organized to use other people’s money; deposits—often credit, not real money—are what they want. The Civil War National Bank System served profiteers but grew too limited; people wanted fairness, profiteers a bigger profit scheme—“Let us see who won.”

Book I — Chapter X — The Federal Reserve Act

File pages 89–100. ~2,380 words.

Before 1907, trust combination and boom strained the old financial system; big interests needed a finance system of their own and control of most banks. Popular dissatisfaction was real but inarticulate. The Act, in Lindbergh’s reading, corners gold and gold certificates, puts finances under big banks, and is a private system for maximum profit from other people’s money—inconsistent with free government. Panics recur (1890–91, 1893–94, 1897, 1903, 1907, 1913, 1921–23); under the new act they can be scientifically created rather than bungled combinations of circumstance.

Book I — Chapter XI — Panics Now Scientifically Created

File pages 101–104. ~790 words.

The present panic is the first scientifically created one, worked as a mathematical problem. At the tail of a panic everything bottoms except money, which booms. Emergency currency legislation furnished banks controlled by speculators until the Reserve could operate fully. For gold plus eligible paper pledged, the Treasury issued multiple paper dollars at a trivial charge; Reserve banks accumulated more than three billion in gold and could end stringency at once if Wall Street profit lay that way.

Book I — Chapter XII — The War Boom

File pages 105–108. ~650 words.

Speculators bought American commodities for European shipment and resold at enormous profit, taking American securities held abroad at bargain prices. War destruction and Allied demand pushed high wages and farm prices; almost everyone expanded. Reserve mechanics—gold pledged for paper at half of one per cent to the government, member rates as the Board directs—concentrated power; at one time over $3.5 billion of such money, more than half the country’s money stock, was in that channel. Farmers, workers, small business, and country banks ran at high speed, little expecting Wall Street to “ring up” the 1920–22 panic.

Book I — Chapter XIII — War Deflation

File pages 109–113. ~970 words.

Before the new panic Wall Street aimed to hit farmers especially hard and to deflate before the costly crop was marketed. Billions made in the boom would be worth less if money stayed cheap. Under Reserve and Esch-Cummins powers the controllers could drive the panic as hard as they wished. People held billions in Liberty Bonds that profiteers had been too busy to buy heavily at issue; panic prices delivered those bonds. Future public and private indebtedness was made to command higher interest for long periods—mortgage on the backs of the living and their children.

Book I — Chapter XIV — Ultimate Breakdown, or Radical Change

File pages 114–125. ~2,400 words.

Boom then collapse of commodity prices (except trust-controlled lines) and the swing in money’s purchasing power set up a future boom for profiteers unless “the radicals” scare it away. The last eight years have doubly mortgaged the future. Mid-argument Lindbergh notes that since earlier chapters were written, bankers and big business find they have nearly killed “the goose that laid the golden egg”—the farmer—and now call conferences. Farmers and wage workers are still exploited; acts such as Lenroot-Anderson are claimed as claims to re-election while parasites take the substance and honest industry the crumbs.

Book I — Chapter XV — The Esch-Cummins Railway Act

File pages 126–130. ~910 words.

No full railway treatise is attempted. Congressmen who voted for Esch-Cummins deny a profit guarantee; Lindbergh answers that after an express six-month guarantee the act in substance charges the Interstate Commerce Commission to fix rates to yield return, and that common ownership links railroads with other great trusts. House and Senate majorities of lawyers and bankers understand the language. The 1922 shopmen’s strike costs could be folded into “cost of maintenance and operation” and recovered in rates—one purpose of the act being to subordinate labor, right or wrong.

Book I — Chapter XVI — “Congressional Investigations”

File pages 131–138. ~1,510 words.

Important acts leave doors open for profiteering; general “stupidity” always favoring wealth is unreasonable as accident. Delayed investigations after damage (loyalty badges to be removed, billions already lost) educate little compared with personal investigation. Speakers and press seek to make others think as they wish them to act; anyone who would know the truth must weigh arguments himself.

Book I — Chapter XVII — The Rules of Congress

File pages 139–148. ~1,900 words.

People need not master every parliamentary trick, but should know that introduction is easy and passage runs a gauntlet. Calendars and leadership fritter time so that bills important to the people and opposed by profiteers never reach consideration. Rules are mainly bad because leaders have served special privilege; the same rules could serve the people under different leaders. A people’s organization removed from party prejudice can check manipulation.

Book I — Chapter XVIII — The Legislative Bloc

File pages 149–155. ~1,400 words.

A bloc or balance of power groups enough votes to force compromise when two larger sides are nearly equal. It may join either side or block action. Special privilege’s luxury leads some to dream the government can make a living for all—an impossibility without briefly exploiting the exploiters. The bloc is a “dog in the manger”: useful if “our” dog, but affirmative action is required to stop serfdom; over-reliance on blocs wastes time and money.

Book I — Chapter XIX — From Lowest to Highest

File pages 156–174. ~3,890 words.

Man’s distinction is intellect over brute force. Nations go to war over disputes smaller than many home problems while neglecting industrial justice. Great individual achievements have not produced a satisfactory plan of justice among persons. Education, schools, and what teachers may say are constrained; children deserve facts at home and in school so the next generation can correct what parents fail to correct—and worse times will come first unless parents learn and force teaching of the truth.

Book I — Chapter XX — Economic Evil, and “Progressives”

File pages 175–186. ~2,400 words.

Those who dominate have no moral right to rule by exploitation; expediency lasts only while the public lacks a remedy. Returning all stolen wealth overnight would not itself found a system. Concrete example: petroleum waste and Standard Oil of New Jersey’s 400 per cent dividend (1922) and another Standard company’s 900 per cent. “Progressive” officeholders drown in small demands, speeches, and constituent errands and cannot do constructive work without risking defeat by constituents who do not understand priorities.

Book I — Chapter XXI — Government; Nation; People

File pages 187–196. ~1,900 words.

Nation and people differ from “government” as administration of political and industrial affairs. True citizenship supports common welfare even while criticizing administration and laws. Arbitrary class lines by wealth and work put a small non-working upper class first; workers alone can reverse enjoyment of the fruits of labor. Mr. and Mrs. Voter must rise above monopolists’ cunning and follow natural law so monopoly serves instead of enslaves.

Book I — Chapter XXII — The Way Out

File pages 197–202. ~840 words.

Abstract subjects and concrete acts have been treated in a human way; readers should draw their own conclusions. Individual action based on truth, not blind following of leaders, is required. Latent powers exist to deal aright; steam and photography show how fast technique moves within living memory. People must create their own conditions and relations with each other and the world rather than let others create conditions that control them.

Book II — Chapter I — The Greatest Game

File pages 205–215. ~2,210 words.

Book I covered general conditions; Book II takes up rules and moves by which workers—including legitimate business—may improve living and approach constitutional individual rights. Business and life advantages are a game: capital confuses and splits workers while exploiting them all; politics writes the rules, so politics matters. Civilized requirements create mutual need; understanding of respective rights is required to break capital’s stranglehold.

Book II — Chapter II — Team-Work for Victory

File pages 216–222. ~1,220 words.

Farmer, wage worker, and legitimate business must stand as one force against separate defeat by capital; relative rights must be adjusted even if not perfect. Organization of all useful elements is required. Federal Reserve and Esch-Cummins showed on their faces how profiteers would use them. Not all is bad; the Constitution still belongs to the people, who may interpret it toward the Preamble’s spirit and use its amending resources through knowledge and united support.

Book II — Chapter III — The American Federations

File pages 223–226. ~650 words.

Existing farm, labor, professional, and business organizations lack common ground to coordinate and adjust differences. Association purposes should be educational and protective against exploitation, waste, and selfish corporate purpose. Present unfair business–politics partnership is again “taxation without representation.” Escape is economic knowledge and honest political control of business while leaving individual initiative that does not trespass the common welfare. Independence of blind political leadership is essential.

Book II — Chapter IV — World Relations

File pages 227–241. ~words in extract band ~2,500+.

Extends the domestic argument to international finance, war settlements, and examples of popular force against governments abroad (Germany, Austria, Italy, Turkey, Hungary, Portugal, Greece, Mexico, and others appear in the OCR). American policy and foreign debts/commerce are treated as continuous with the profiteer problem at home; the useful groups’ federation logic is applied beyond national borders. (OCR density and running headers are heavier in this band; use raw pages 227–241 for quotation.)

Book II — Chapter V — By the People—For the People

File pages 242–252. Closes the program with the claim that government must be made to execute the people’s will, not merely voice it weakly. Direct popular energy, informed federation, and refusal of machine rule are restated as the path from Book I’s diagnosis to durable self-rule under the Constitution’s spirit.

Limitations and Caveats

  • Partisan primary tract, not neutral economic history; claims about Reserve ownership, panic design, and congressional motive are advocacy framed as exposé.
  • OCR quality requires checking wiki/raw/lindbergh-economic-pinch.txt (or original page images) before long quotation; hyphens and headers break many lines.
  • Person disambiguation: author is Charles August Lindbergh (1859–1924), Minnesota congressman and father of the aviator; do not merge with 1927 flight or 1930s–40s America First material without a separate source.
  • Dated statistics (rail capital, gold stocks, dividend percentages) are 1922–23 polemical figures as printed, not modern revised series.
