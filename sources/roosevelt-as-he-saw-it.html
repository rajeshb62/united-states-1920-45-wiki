As He Saw It

Author: Elliott Roosevelt

Completeness Assessment

The PDF extract is complete enough for wiki use. Front matter: title, copyright, dedication, Eleanor Roosevelt’s Foreword (pp. vii–ix), Contents, Introduction. Body: eleven chapters (1 Texas to Argentia through 11 Conclusion) matching the imprint Contents, then Index. OCR shows typical scan artifacts (hyphenation across lines, occasional garbled characters such as \\V for W, mixed running heads), but chapter blocks are continuous and narrative-readable. Elliott was not at Yalta; Chapter 10 reconstructs from Anna Boettiger, Harry Hopkins, letters from FDR, and the official trip log—stated in the text.

Overview

Elliott Roosevelt frames the book as only incidentally about the war and primarily about the peace. He writes in 1946 under the spur of Churchill’s Fulton, Missouri speech, Security Council sessions at Hunter College, the American atom-bomb stockpile, and what he calls renascent power politics and broken promises. His qualifications are biological (FDR’s son) and situational: he was most often available as wartime presidential aide—Argentia while based at Gander; Casablanca while operating from Algeria; Cairo–Teheran while headquarters remained in Tunisia—and sat in on formal and informal conferences, then compared notes with his father at night. He draws on official conference logs plus his own contemporaneous notes more than unaided memory.

For this wiki the book is the wartime high-diplomacy spine from a family aide’s seat: Atlantic Charter and Lend-Lease priorities; Casablanca strategy, French politics, and “unconditional surrender”; Chiang at Cairo; Teheran and OVERLORD versus Mediterranean/Turkish diversions; Anzio as Churchill’s “end run”; Yalta as fleshing out Teheran after Dumbarton Oaks friction; and a 1946 Conclusion blaming U.S./British departure from Big Three unity (Greece, Turkey/Dardanelles, Iran oil, atom secrecy, military diplomacy). It pairs with Groves for the atomic-production/operations track, with Allen’s Since Yesterday for the 1930s approach to war, and later with Conant for scientific mobilization. Treat as partisan contemporary memoir, not multi-archival synthesis—cross-check contested dialogue and Soviet policy claims when building event pages.

Narrative Source Flow

Foreword (Eleanor Roosevelt)

Lines 42–125. ~690 words.

FDR wanted individual impressions of the war and peace-planning recorded for better understanding among peoples. He sought family companionship on trips when duty allowed; James as secretary is recalled as comfort despite political cost. Eleanor was not on the conference trips; she heard first-night reunion accounts. She presents Elliott’s book as one observer’s firsthand material for future historians, while noting that each personality colors the same facts differently.

Introduction

Lines 146–323. ~1,490 words.

Incidentally a war book, designed more importantly to shed light on the peace. Span: roughly war’s outbreak through shortly after Yalta. Decision to write is recent—Fulton, Hunter College Security Council, atom stockpile, disunity, broken promises, “renascent power politics.” Trust or distrust of Russia is molded by scare headlines rather than Russia’s contribution to victory. Elliott stakes an individual space among the postwar flood of generals’ and correspondents’ books: as son he could hear FDR talk “as though he were talking out loud to himself”; as aide he ushered Churchill, Stalin, the Chiangs, Combined Chiefs, de Gaulle, Giraud, Hopkins, Molotov, and others, then heard FDR’s impressions. Only Yalta found him unable to be at FDR’s side. He claims knowledge of bargains and promises for postwar peace structure—and writes because he has seen them violated. Audience: those who hold FDR the wartime architect of United Nations unity and believe that path has been deliberately forsaken.

Chapter 1 — Texas to Argentia

Lines 324–828. ~4,310 words.

Opens with Munich weekend 1938: Elliott about to launch Texas radio stations, pumps FDR on whether war will wreck business. FDR will not guarantee no war; sooner or later Britain and France will decide Hitler has gone too far; U.S. working hard to stay out; advises Elliott to go ahead with the radio project. Path from civilian enterprise through service entry to Newfoundland reconnaissance duty. Summons to Argentia: at first thought a naval maneuver; Friday before lunch finds FDR aboard for a multi-day stay; neck size matches father’s shirts for the Atlantic Charter signing. Sets family and military positioning for Chapter 2.

Chapter 2 — The Atlantic Charter

Lines 829–1724. ~7,520 words.

Franklin Jr. and Elliott receive temporary aide aiguillettes. FDR delights in throwing White House correspondents off the scent (supposed fishing off Fundy/Potomac to Augusta) under agreement with Churchill for no press coverage—then Churchill arrives with a press retinue “not very well disguised as Ministry of Information officials,” the first of several such surprises. Meeting at sea suits two naval men who had last met in that capacity in 1919; security and rumor control keep it out of Washington. Substance: Lend-Lease allocation fights in afternoon military sessions—British pressure for more to the United Kingdom and less to the Soviet Union; Marshall, King, and Arnold insist on keeping both warrior nations supplied. Time and production still a gamble; American destiny framed as in firm hands striving to keep the country at peace while protecting long-range interests. Charter text and public announcement close the conference arc as Elliott returns to duty.

Chapter 3 — From Argentia to Casablanca

Lines 1725–2175. ~3,760 words.

Post-Argentia: squadron ordered home; Elliott seeks navigation school (Kelly Field) against stateside complacency he blames on a section of the press (Chicago Tribune, Hearst, New York Daily News, Washington Times-Herald, Scripps-Howard). Pearl Harbor weekend at the Fort Worth ranch; wartime acceleration; overseas to North Africa after TORCH. Hard operational grind at Maison Blanche near Algiers. 11 January 1943: ordered to report to Eisenhower’s Chief of Staff—bridge into Casablanca as presidential aide while still a serving air officer.

Chapter 4 — Casablanca Conference

Lines 2176–4204. ~16,600 words.

Largest chapter. Arrival after TORCH: Jean Bart in harbor, Patton’s traffic, Anfa Camp security under Mike Reilly amid residual enemy agents, Spanish Morocco proximity, and a recent Nazi air raid. French political tangle: Giraud, de Gaulle’s delayed appearance, FDR’s pointed pressure on Churchill over de Gaulle. Military strategy debates (ROUNDUP/Mediterranean weight, production, timing). FDR’s jeep inspection with Patton. Extended father–son evening talk: Empire, Atlantic Charter obligations, Asia and Europe after victory—“not senior partner”; Britain must not hang on to “archaic, medieval Empire ideas.” Birth of the public phrase “unconditional surrender” in FDR’s press-handling (no exclamation point in Elliott’s telling)—with FDR in “fine fettle” over the formula. Operational and political Casablanca as hinge between Argentia idealism and later Cairo–Teheran command decisions.

Chapter 5 — From Casablanca to Cairo

Lines 4205–4755. ~4,600 words.

Stalingrad as tonic; photographic reconnaissance for HUSKY (Sicily) at extreme cost—stripped unarmed aircraft, ~20 percent monthly plane loss; within ninety days fewer than ten percent of original ninety-four pilots answer roll call. Occasional bridge with Eisenhower, Butcher, “Tex” Lee; Pantelleria surrender to air forces. Sicily and Italian mainland work; summons toward the Cairo meetings as FDR’s party enters the Near East theater.

Chapter 6 — Cairo I

Lines 4756–5701. ~8,020 words.

Flight Tunis–Egypt; Nile green against desert; Mena House and Kirk villa; Chiangs and Churchill already present; Reilly’s barbed wire and assassination worry in a crowded political city. FDR breakfasting in bed; Combined Chiefs and political sessions with Generalissimo and Madame Chiang; China theater supply, Burma, and postwar Asia threads. Military planning for Europe continues in parallel with Pacific/China agenda. Sets personnel and issues for the flight to Teheran.

Chapter 7 — Teheran Conference

Lines 5702–6653. ~8,040 words.

Seventy-seven Americans view Teheran from the air—compromise site forced by Stalin’s one-day-from-Moscow requirement; former Axis espionage HQ; open-ditch water/sewage and disease risk. Security shared with Soviet services. Core military issue: OVERLORD—timing, weight, command—versus further Mediterranean exploitation and bringing Turkey into the war. Stalin sides with the American cross-Channel emphasis against diversions Elliott associates with Churchill. Gift symbolism (Stalingrad sword), plenary and dinner diplomacy, and FDR’s effort to mediate British–Soviet tension. Elliott treats Teheran as projecting the overall postwar and strategic picture later only detailed at Yalta.

Chapter 8 — Cairo II

Lines 6654–7106. ~3,940 words.

Brief return to Tunis to close La Marsa rear HQ and move north to Italy; word to Eisenhower that OVERLORD is definitely scheduled and that Russians opposed further Mediterranean exploitation and Turkish entry—command of OVERLORD still unsettled in Eisenhower’s hearing. Back to Cairo: contest over whose plane brings Turkey’s İsmet İnönü from Adana (John Boettiger wins for the American side). İnönü conversations; further Combined Chiefs work; detective-story downtime for FDR between plenaries. Closes the November–December 1943 conference cycle.

Chapter 9 — From Cairo–Teheran to Yalta

Lines 7107–7713. ~5,240 words.

Italy scuttlebutt; skepticism toward Anzio (“end run” near Rome) after Combined Chiefs strategy in Egypt/Iran; orders to Spaatz in England to reorganize Eighth/Ninth Air Force reconnaissance for invasion support. Later learns Churchill personally insisted on SHINGLE. January 1944 arrival in Britain; praise for RAF opposite numbers despite earlier criticisms of some British warmakers. Build-up to OVERLORD operations; stateside leaves and White House nights as available. Bridge to Yalta chapter without Elliott’s physical presence at the Crimea.

Chapter 10 — Yalta Conference

Lines 7714–8159. ~3,790 words.

Explicitly reconstructed: not present at Malta, Yalta, or Great Bitter Lake; sources are sister Anna, Harry Hopkins, FDR’s letters, and official trip log—accounts said to tally on major particulars. Longest trip in time (~five weeks away); eight formal Big Three meetings plus many informal talks in eight Crimean days; more advisers than ever. Elliott’s thesis: not the war’s most important high-level conference because principal decisions had already been taken in Washington, Cairo, and Teheran—Yalta filled in specifics after Dumbarton Oaks showed lower levels not seeing eye to eye. Unanimity again put “flesh on the skeleton” of Teheran. Counterfactual: had FDR lived, further Big Three meetings beyond Potsdam would have followed. Personal reactions and health notes filtered through family and Hopkins.

Chapter 11 — Conclusion

Lines 8160–8574. ~3,540 words.

Job of forging global peace was begun under FDR and since his death has been prejudiced or is “fast being lost.” Evidence: no peace (Lippmann); advanced U.S. air bases and rocket planes in Germany; jealous refusal to share the atom “secret” that Manhattan scientists say is no secret; cocktail-party talk of war with the Soviet Union “preferably before 1948.” Elliott’s causal claim: the United States and Britain first shook the mailed fist and abrogated collective decisions; the U.S. vacated mediator role between Britain and the USSR and lined up behind Britain—Greece (British shooting of Greek anti-fascists; election recognized as democratic), Turkey/Dardanelles, Iran oil and Security Council theater. Blames “a small group of willful men”: State Department career men FDR never trusted, reactionaries of both parties, irresponsible press attacking the veto, and officers shrinking foreign policy to the atom bomb. Favors large forces only as UN security instrument under Big Three unity. Closes with democratic lesson: great Presidents respond to articulated popular will; citizens must push government back onto FDR’s charted path.

Index

Lines 8575–9059. ~3,350 words.

Name and subject index (persons, conferences, OVERLORD, unconditional surrender, production, theaters) supporting point retrieval across the memoir.

Coverage Guide

Mine first as 1941–45 conference diplomacy and 1946 peace polemic from an aide-son. Chapters 2, 4, 6–8, and 10 for Argentia, Casablanca, Cairo, Teheran, and Yalta event pages; Chapter 4 for unconditional surrender and French leadership; Chapter 7 for OVERLORD decision politics; Chapter 9 for Anzio/SHINGLE as Churchill initiative in this telling; Chapter 11 for early Cold War contest over FDR’s legacy (Fulton, Iran, Greece, atom). Cross-check all quoted FDR/Churchill/Stalin dialogue and anti-imperial / pro-unity theses against official FRUS minutes and other memoirs before locking narrative wiki claims. Eleanor’s Foreword is framing, not conference evidence. Yalta chapter is second-hand by admission.

Notes on Scope

1946 contemporary memoir and political argument by a participant-observer with unmatched informal access and clear advocacy (preserve FDR’s Big Three peace architecture; criticize Churchillian empire, State careerists, and early Cold War alignment against the USSR). Strong on atmosphere, seating, security logistics, and remembered private talk; weak as sole authority on formal agreed texts, military staff detail, or Soviet motives. OCR noise requires care when quoting; prefer paraphrase with line anchors or verify against a cleaner edition when precision matters.
